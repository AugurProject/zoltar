Prediction markets

How Statoblast works

Follow one question: “Will the fictional Aurora spacecraft launch before 31 December 2030?” Statoblast creates a collateralized market with Yes, No, and Invalid shares, resolves ordinary disagreement locally, and uses Zoltar branches only when local resolution cannot produce a decision or another fork interrupts the pool.

1. Mental model

#

Statoblast is the market layer. Each SecurityPool ledger serves one question in one universe. Separate portfolios keep separate ledgers even when their question and configuration match. Each ledger holds ETH settlement collateral, tracks vault owners' underwriting commitments, and mints, migrates, and settles shares. Zoltar owns the questions, universes, REP, and forks underneath it; Trading and OpenOracle sit beside it. The system overview introduces all four.

2. Normal market lifecycle

#
  1. Register the example question in Zoltar and deploy its SecurityPool in one universe.
  2. Vault owners deposit REP; traders deposit ETH to mint complete sets.
  3. Traders may exchange shares through an exchange such as Trading.
  4. After the question ends, the first REP deposit on an outcome opens the Escalation Game.
  5. An unchallenged report wins three days later; a contested game runs until one outcome is the strict leader at its deadline. Holders then redeem winning shares from the pool's settlement collateral.

4. Local resolution

#

The game opens with the first REP deposit after the question ends. REP-backed balances then accumulate behind Invalid, Yes, and No. Their median is binding capital and determines the moving deadline. After that deadline, exactly one strict balance leader produces the local winner. Winning shares redeem from that pool.

If two outcomes reach the non-decision threshold, the game does not choose between them. Anyone can then fork the universe on the market question through Statoblast, which pays the fork threshold from the REP held by the pool and the game. See the Escalation Game explanation for the required-support curve, deadlines, payouts, and how games continue across forks.

5. Forks and migration

#

An unresolved operational pool can enter the fork path in two ways. Its own Escalation Game can reach non-decision and fork Zoltar on the market question; in this example, that creates Invalid, Yes, and No children. A fork of the pool's universe on another question can also interrupt it, in which case the children represent that categorical or scalar question's valid answers. A finalized pool remains settled and rejects later fork migration.

Migration is open for a migration window of eight weeks, counted from the call that puts the parent pool into fork mode. A vault's parent backing claim, underwriting commitment, and proportional pool collateral are routed into one selected child pool. The same parent vault migration cannot be reused across siblings. Eligible outcome-share balances work differently: users can materialize branch-specific ledger claims in multiple selected child pools. That reproduces claims, not ETH; see what Zoltar does not guarantee.

If a child receives too little ETH, it can sell child REP through a one-week Truth Auction, which anyone can start once the migration window has closed. Full demand can repair the shortfall; weak demand can leave redemption below face value.

6. Fees, underwriting, and liquidations

#

REP deposits do not create underwriting commitments; each vault owner sets one separately. A vault's share of total commitments sets its share of the pool's settlement collateral obligations and of the open-interest fees that outstanding collateral pays.

New complete sets can be minted only before the question ends, while total commitments cover the resulting collateral and no Escalation Game has opened. Minting needs no oracle price or backing check, because vault owners already answer for their full commitments (invariant BAL-03).

A vault whose REP no longer backs its commitment can be liquidated: some or all of its commitment and backing move to an authorized receiver vault. See vault liquidation.

7. Managing several questions

#

A SecurityPoolPortfolio combines depositors' REP into one reserve for future question allocations. Nontransferable reserve shares represent only unallocated REP. When capital is allocated, a separate position records its owners; that position's REP, gains, losses, fees and obligations belong to those owners. Reserve shares and allocation claims are both separate from the outcome shares bought by traders. A buyer selects a question and an amount, without selecting individual depositors.

Each question keeps its own SecurityPool ledger, settlement ETH, outcome shares, dispute stakes, and repair auction. REP allocated to one ledger cannot simultaneously back another. While reserve ownership is unchanged, an allocation can receive further backing and return permitted surplus to the common reserve. After ownership changes, retained capital can keep serving the same question. If it cannot fund another purchase, current reserve owners fund a new allocation. Minting maintains only the selected question's reusable allocation.

The portfolio creator fixes a maximum remaining question horizon. Anyone may register an open question within that horizon before the origin universe forks. Registration alone moves no REP. Paid complete-set purchases trigger automatic allocation under the common backing policy. Every portfolio purchase needs a settled fresh REP price; allocation, fee payment and minting either succeed together or revert.

Every purchase through the portfolio pays a fee based on the larger of its standard REP backing requirement and the REP newly deposited for it. Reusing existing backing does not remove the fee. If a purchase also replenishes an older allocation's backing shortfall, the fee covers that additional capital, including liquid REP retained by historical owners. Buyers see the fee alongside the ETH funding their shares, set a maximum fee budget and receive any unused budget back. Redeeming complete sets does not refund the purchase fee. The question ledger's ongoing time-based fees also remain. Direct native minting against existing commitments follows the native ledger's rules; the portfolio purchase fee belongs to this automatic-allocation entry point. Exact amounts and recipients are in the underwriting reference.

Deposits join the idle reserve and future allocations. For example, if Alice allocates 60 REP and retains 40 REP in the reserve before Bob deposits, Bob acquires none of Alice's 60 REP position. Withdrawing reserve shares returns proportional idle REP while preserving earlier allocation ownership and earned fees.

An owner can collect released capital directly: their claim processes selected allocations for all remaining owners, then pays their own accumulated REP and fees. They do not need to wait for another processor. Anyone may also process an allocation separately, and owners can collect already-credited proceeds without processing it again. The accounting records ranges over which that owner's reserve share balance stayed constant; new questions and other people's deposits do not create more ranges for them. Recoveries can arrive in any order and remain claimable after the owner withdraws all reserve shares. Owners with many balance changes can collect selected ranges in separate transactions.

This removes the need for every depositor to claim every question during ordinary cash recovery. A selected allocation credits all remaining owners, but processing still costs work per allocation and can recur as more capital becomes available. Owners choose how many allocations and personal ranges to include in each transaction. Part of each new allocation's purchase fee funds one reward when its source backing is recovered or fully transferred, including by a shared fork migration. This does not mean its child backing is withdrawable yet. Fees and available capital limit manufactured demand; they do not prove it unprofitable. There is no total question or allocation cap.

An owner who needs direct control for disputes or an individual fork migration can instead transfer a selected position into their personal recovery account. Only that owner can initiate the transfer. Their part is removed from future shared recovery, while previously credited proceeds remain claimable. Other owners retain their positions and may continue using their retained capital for that question. Taking individual control creates additional recovery ranges only for the owner choosing it.

After ordinary resolution, the game reserves the REP owed to all winning claims and their required burns, retires losing escrow together, and releases the surplus when backing is recovered. Other reporters do not need to claim first. A vault's own winning dispute stake remains locked until its owner claims it. Personal recovery accounts can request runoff, allowing anyone to process recoverable positions for their owner; only that owner can consent to forfeiting a possible residual claim for an exit before finality. Coverage, escrow and fork obligations still constrain actual withdrawals. A question's admission horizon is not a promised withdrawal date, and outcome shareholders need not redeem before final REP withdrawal because their ETH stays reserved in the question ledger.

All question ledgers in the same portfolio and universe use one REP/ETH price feed. Its report funding floor counts each ledger's larger value of commitments or settlement collateral, so funded questions can raise reporting costs for everyone. A bad or delayed report affects price-dependent operations together. The floor is sampled when a report opens; it does not continuously cap commitments during the fresh-price window. Different portfolios and child universes have separate feeds.

When the origin universe forks, the reserve stops new deposits and purchases. Each owner can join shared migration by choosing a branch for selected ownership ranges during a four-week choice window. The choice can be switched or revoked until the cutoff; a selected range cannot also be extracted individually. After the cutoff, anyone can migrate an allocation once for all its owners who chose the same branch. Other branches need their own migration, but do not need to finish first. No manager or depositor vote selects a common winning child.

Shared migration preserves allocation-time ownership. Already released parent REP and pre-fork fees remain payable through the source ledger. Child REP and later fees belong only to that branch's owners and use a separate recovery ledger. Owners collect across their personal ranges after the child permits recovery; they do not each repeat the native migration. Questions finalized before the fork retain ordinary source-REP recovery. Child ledgers share a new feed that starts stale because that branch has a different REP token.

Joining the shared choice is optional. Unselected ranges retain individual extraction and migration through each pool's eight-week native window, measured from that pool's fork activation. Missing the earlier shared cutoff does not choose a default branch or shorten this native window. Missing the native deadline can lose the owner's backing entitlement. An owner can prepare the child and perform the required transactions themselves; the system does not guarantee that an outside processor will do it profitably.

The same grouping works if a child universe forks again. Owners can record that descendant choice before all its allocations have arrived. Its window uses a lower bound from the incoming native migration period, preventing an early child-universe fork from expiring the window immediately. Missing ancestor migration or an unfinished native child phase still requires explicit progress on those allocations; no call scans the whole ancestry or portfolio. Work remains linear in allocations processed for each selected branch, so the choice window alone does not guarantee that an arbitrarily large portfolio finishes before native deadlines. See the shared-fork reference for timing and interfaces, and the security assumptions for the broader limits.

8. Security assumptions and non-guarantees

#
  • REP-backed incentives do not prove objective truth; users and applications decide which branches retain activity.
  • OpenOracle depends on timely, profitable correction of inaccurate REP/ETH reports.
  • A fork gives each child only the ETH that migrates to it (Zoltar's non-guarantees).
  • ETH pushed into a pool outside its accounting is not counted as collateral. Steps that anyone may trigger, such as forking or starting an auction, still need someone to send the transaction.
  • Spot prices from trading pools are manipulable and are never protocol oracles.

Normative assumptions and guarantees are listed in the security model and invariant catalog.

Design rationale

Statoblast's ancestry includes the Arctic Tern Oracle, Sisyphean Exchange, Partial Colored Coins, and Isonzo. Those research ideas influenced the design; the deployed contracts govern current behavior.
