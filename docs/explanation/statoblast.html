Prediction markets

How Statoblast works

Follow one question: “Will the fictional Aurora spacecraft launch before 31 December 2030?” Statoblast creates a collateralized market with Yes, No, and Invalid shares, resolves ordinary disagreement locally, and uses Zoltar branches only when local resolution cannot produce a decision or another fork interrupts the pool.

1. Mental model

#

Statoblast is the market layer. Each SecurityPool ledger serves one question in one universe. Separate portfolios keep separate ledgers even when their question and configuration match. Each ledger holds ETH settlement collateral, tracks vault owners' underwriting commitments, and mints, migrates, and settles shares. Zoltar owns the questions, universes, REP, and forks underneath it; Trading and OpenOracle sit beside it. The system overview introduces all four.

2. Normal market lifecycle

#
  1. Register the example question in Zoltar and deploy its SecurityPool in one universe.
  2. Vault owners deposit REP; traders deposit ETH to mint complete sets.
  3. Traders may exchange shares through an exchange such as Trading.
  4. After the question ends, the first REP deposit on an outcome opens the Escalation Game.
  5. An unchallenged report wins three days later; a contested game runs until one outcome is the strict leader at its deadline. Holders then redeem winning shares from the pool's settlement collateral.

4. Local resolution

#

The game opens with the first REP deposit after the question ends. REP-backed balances then accumulate behind Invalid, Yes, and No. Their median is binding capital and determines the moving deadline. After that deadline, exactly one strict balance leader produces the local winner. Winning shares redeem from that pool.

If two outcomes reach the non-decision threshold, the game does not choose between them. Anyone can then fork the universe on the market question through Statoblast, which pays the fork threshold from the REP held by the pool and the game. See the Escalation Game explanation for the required-support curve, deadlines, payouts, and how games continue across forks.

5. Forks and migration

#

An unresolved operational pool can enter the fork path in two ways. Its own Escalation Game can reach non-decision and fork Zoltar on the market question; in this example, that creates Invalid, Yes, and No children. A fork of the pool's universe on another question can also interrupt it, in which case the children represent that categorical or scalar question's valid answers. A finalized pool remains settled and rejects later fork migration.

Migration is open for a migration window of eight weeks, counted from the call that puts the parent pool into fork mode. A vault's parent backing claim, underwriting commitment, and proportional pool collateral are routed into one selected child pool. The same parent vault migration cannot be reused across siblings. Eligible outcome-share balances work differently: users can materialize branch-specific ledger claims in multiple selected child pools. That reproduces claims, not ETH; see what Zoltar does not guarantee.

If a child receives too little ETH, it can sell child REP through a one-week Truth Auction, which anyone can start once the migration window has closed. Full demand can repair the shortfall; weak demand can leave redemption below face value.

6. Fees, underwriting, and liquidations

#

REP deposits do not create underwriting commitments; each vault owner sets one separately. A vault's share of total commitments sets its share of the pool's settlement collateral obligations and of the open-interest fees that outstanding collateral pays.

New complete sets can be minted only before the question ends, while total commitments cover the resulting collateral and no Escalation Game has opened. Minting needs no oracle price or backing check, because vault owners already answer for their full commitments (invariant BAL-03).

A vault whose REP no longer backs its commitment can be liquidated: some or all of its commitment and backing move to an authorized receiver vault. See vault liquidation.

7. Managing several questions

#

A SecurityPoolPortfolio is a multi-question pool with one REP/ETH price feed per universe. Its question ledgers share the origin universe, security multiplier, and oracle priority fee. Each underwriter can create an account to manage allocations across them. Its owner is the wallet or smart account that created it. Each question keeps a separate SecurityPool ledger, settlement collateral, shares, fees, dispute stakes, and repair auction. Different portfolios have separate ledgers and feeds even when their rules match. Registering a question gives it no claim on anyone's capital.

One accepted report refreshes every question in the same pool and universe; admitting another question does not reset that price. The report funding floor counts each active ledger's larger value of standing commitments or share collateral. Adding funded commitments can therefore raise the cost of the next report for everyone using that feed. A bad or delayed report affects their price-dependent operations together. Existing oracle assumptions still apply: the funding floor is measured when a report opens, and does not cap commitments added during its fresh-price window. The initiating question owns the report's bounded operation callback; another question can use the price after settlement.

The portfolio creator fixes a maximum time until a question ends. Creation deploys the pool and its feed; a second transaction adds its first question. If that admission fails or is cancelled, the existing pool can still accept an eligible question later. Anyone can register another question while its remaining time is within that horizon and the origin universe has not forked. Questions can have different start and end dates. Registration alone moves no REP.

An owner can enable automatic underwriting with an idle REP reserve, a minimum health factor, and fees for reserving capital. The health factor sets the required margin above native backing requirements. Anyone buying complete sets can then pay to route the needed commitment increase through that account. The transaction checks a settled fresh REP price, deposits the required REP, and mints shares for the buyer atomically. Only the missing capacity is allocated.

The buyer funds reservation fees separately from share collateral and sets a maximum fee budget; unused budget returns to the buyer. Each allocation charges the owner's rate on the additional REP reserved, including the native minimum backing, or the owner's minimum ETH fee if higher. This minimum can compensate the owner for handling small positions and their later exit costs. The fee belongs to that owner's account and is not returned when the buyer redeems shares. The owner can withdraw collected ETH without disabling automation. Native open-interest fees remain separate. These charges price access to capital but impose no per-question or per-creator limits: buyers can still manufacture demand or concentrate an opted-in account in any eligible question.

Requesting withdrawal starts full-account runoff: automatic allocation stops immediately, idle origin REP returns to the owner, and anyone may process recoverable question positions for that owner. Each account has its own funds, so the withdrawal journal does not impose first-in-first-out priority across depositors. Cancelling runoff does not restart automation. Owner-selected native calls revoke automation too, but can still change positions; previously staged native operations retain their existing expiry and execution rules.

An ordinary Escalation Game is one that has not continued through a fork. Once its principal and escrow claims settle, any REP left in the game can be returned to its question pool. This residual REP increases each remaining account's REP claim in proportion to its share of the pool backing. Runoff waits for those outstanding game claims before withdrawing backing, preserving the account's share of this residual. Final redemption collects available residual first. The owner can separately consent to early exit that gives up later residual; native coverage and escrow checks still apply.

The horizon bounds new question exposure, not the time needed to resolve a dispute. While an outcome remains unresolved, outstanding collateral can still lock commitments. Share holders need not redeem before final REP withdrawal: their ETH remains reserved in the question pool. Losses reduce the REP returned. Fork migration, dispute settlement, and the transactions needed to progress them can extend the wait.

Owners can also allocate manually or use the optional executor policy with explicit budgets. Those are separate authorizations; enabling one automation mode replaces the other. Neither a higher health factor nor separate question ledgers removes price risk or makes the same REP available to back two questions.

If one question forks the universe, each unresolved pool follows its existing migration path. The account owner selects migrations per question; the portfolio has no global child choice. Ledgers in the same child universe share a new feed, which starts stale because that branch has a different REP token. Keeping the ledgers separate preserves their allocation rules, while the existing security assumptions still apply. This composition is not a proof of those economic assumptions. See the portfolio trust boundaries for account authority and pool discovery.

8. Security assumptions and non-guarantees

#
  • REP-backed incentives do not prove objective truth; users and applications decide which branches retain activity.
  • OpenOracle depends on timely, profitable correction of inaccurate REP/ETH reports.
  • A fork gives each child only the ETH that migrates to it (Zoltar's non-guarantees).
  • ETH pushed into a pool outside its accounting is not counted as collateral. Steps that anyone may trigger, such as forking or starting an auction, still need someone to send the transaction.
  • Spot prices from trading pools are manipulable and are never protocol oracles.

Normative assumptions and guarantees are listed in the security model and invariant catalog.

Design rationale

Statoblast's ancestry includes the Arctic Tern Oracle, Sisyphean Exchange, Partial Colored Coins, and Isonzo. Those research ideas influenced the design; the deployed contracts govern current behavior.
