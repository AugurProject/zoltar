Statoblast security model

Statoblast and Zoltar enforce accounting, authorization, and lifecycle rules onchain. Their economic safety and practical liveness still depend on the participant, market, asset, deployment, client, data, Ethereum, and cryptographic assumptions below.

An assumption is not a guarantee enforced by the contracts. Each entry says what must remain true, what can fail if it does not, and where the related mechanics are explained. The Statoblast and Zoltar invariants catalog lists contract-enforced properties and their implementation evidence.

Security-model orientation

The assets and state at stake are REP, ERC-20 settlement collateral, outcome shares, vault backing, Escalation Game deposits, child-universe accounting, and the integrity of event-derived state.

Boundary Examples
Trusted protocol components Zoltar, question ledgers, share tokens, shared price feeds, question forkers, and their configured delegates.
External dependencies OpenOracle, collateral/REP token behavior, Ethereum execution and finality, RPCs, wallets, and proof data.
Untrusted actors Traders, vault owners, liquidators, disputers, bidders, settlers, keepers, and indexers.
Failure outcomes Reverted calls, delayed transitions, underfunded child repair, stale prices, incorrect external data, or unreconstructable history.

Which assumptions support my claim?

  • A wrong local outcome can be challenged in time. Check timely evidence, REP for a competing deposit, inclusion before the deadline.
  • A harmful oracle price can be corrected. Check a funded corrector, an independent incentive, a reliable reference market, timely inclusion.
  • A truthful child can cover its collateral shortfall. Check retained backing value, bidder demand and the opening and closing sale conditions. See EG02 on partial repair.
  • An eligible public settlement advances in time. Check an executor, timely inclusion.

For the reads, deadlines, and stop conditions behind these decisions, see Monitor security assumptions.

Participation and coordination

These assumptions explain why someone performs the economically useful action instead of merely being permitted to perform it.

  1. A01 Participants act on risk-adjusted economic incentives

    The economic model assumes participants weigh expected gains against costs, delay, and risk when deciding whether to act.

    Failure boundary: A nominally profitable liquidation, dispute, bid, migration, or fork defense may not happen when its risk-adjusted return is unattractive.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: economic roles, liquidation incentives, auction clearing, and oracle incentives.

  2. A02 Privately motivated actions cover their costs

    When timely action depends on private incentives, at least one participant expects the value they earn or protect to exceed their transaction, coordination, and capital costs.

    Failure boundary: An action can be technically executable but economically abandoned before its deadline.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: Collateral Repair Auction lifecycle, oracle gas parameters, and Escalation Game lifecycle.

  3. A03 Price discovery and contests attract independent participants

    Competitive auction pricing depends on independent bidders, external trading prices depend on independent traders, and contesting a local outcome requires someone willing to fund an opposing outcome.

    Failure boundary: Thin or controlled participation can remove price discovery, competitive bidding, or an opposing outcome deposit.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: economic roles, fork coordination, and auction participation.

  4. A04 Participants can mobilize REP to raise an alarm

    A participant can deliver enough REP for a competing local deposit before the game closes. A further reply after a change of leader requires enough time at the new median stake to bring more REP into the game. The reward cap encourages a stake margin, but sets no minimum reply time.

    Failure boundary: A wrong local outcome can become final before better-capitalized participants enter.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: escalation resolution, escalation accounting, and escalation guardrails.

  5. A05 Participants value continued access to the supported Statoblast lineage

    Users and integrations whose branch choices determine durable use would lose enough by abandoning the supported lineage to deter that choice. Estimate what they lose relative to their best available alternative. The model uses the lineage’s configured backing multiplier as its deterrence hurdle for the same secured exposure.

    ValueStatoblast access × BPS_DENOMINATOR ≥ statoblastSecurityMultiplierBps × Valuesecured

    Compare one lineage’s continued-use value and secured value at the same pre-attack time. Divide the configured basis points by 10,000 to obtain the multiplier.

    Failure boundary: Participants may rationally abandon, grief, or coordinate away from a lineage whose continued-use value is too small.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: fork security, fork migration, and Statoblast security boundary.

  6. A06 At least one executor advances every required public transition

    For each required public transition, at least one caller can pay for and submit the transaction in time. Examples include fork activation, auction finalization and oracle settlement or callback recovery. A takeover also needs a willing, funded receiver. Holder-directed migration is covered by A11.

    Failure boundary: Permissionless state can remain stalled even though no contract authorization prevents progress.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: lifecycle liveness, fork migration guardrails, and oracle guardrails.

Forks, truth, value, and liquidity

These assumptions connect onchain branching and accounting to offchain truth judgments, asset value, liquidity, and migration.

  1. A07 Truthful-child REP retains enough backing value

    For one protected Statoblast origin lineage, let Vparent be its pre-fork REP backing value and Vchild the corresponding backing value in the supported truthful child after fork haircuts, at the repair decision horizon. Both use the same collateral numeraire; unrelated external collateral and REP in other branches are excluded. The multiplier m is the origin's immutable statoblastSecurityMultiplierBps / 10_000.

    Vchild ≥ Vparentm

    At 20_000 BPS, this bound requires retaining at least half the parent backing value; at 30_000 BPS, at least one third. At the minimum admission ratio, that retained value equals the secured collateral before realization costs. This is a valuation assumption, not an onchain parent/child price check or a guarantee of full repair; auction participation and conversion costs are covered by A12.

    Failure boundary: Insufficient retained backing value can leave a truthful child's collateral shortfall unrepaired.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: security pool fork migration. Rationale: REP backing and open interest.

  2. A08 REP market capitalization approximates economic value

    When an assessment uses market capitalization to estimate REP discounted cash flow for A14, it assumes the estimate is accurate enough to preserve the comparison with open interest at the same time and scope.

    Failure boundary: Thin, manipulated, or speculative pricing can overstate the economic value securing open interest.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: REP economics and economic backing claims.

  3. A09 Users value the universes they judge truthful

    Users prefer the universe or universes they judge truthful, so economic activity and durable asset value concentrate there. This assumption describes the durable allocation of value after users coordinate; A07 bounds backing value at the repair decision horizon.

    Failure boundary: Coordination on a false branch, or durable fragmentation across branches, defeats the value-concentration security argument.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: branch non-guarantees and security pool fork migration.

  4. A10 Participants can access timely outcome evidence

    Participants can obtain and interpret reliable outcome evidence early enough to fund and submit a response before the relevant deadline.

    Failure boundary: Missing, delayed, or conflicting evidence can split honest capital or let a false local outcome become final.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: question encoding, invalid outcomes, and local resolution.

  5. A11 Migration is operationally practical

    REP holders can migrate into a child without a native REP migration deadline. Underwriters must choose their branch before the branch-choice cutoff. Share holders must be able to call migration for their own balances once the destination branch is active. Each path depends on the holder or its custodian supporting that branch.

    Failure boundary: Supported branches can remain illiquid or undercollateralized because economic claims and backing do not move there.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: REP splitting, security pool fork migration, and share migration guardrails.

  6. A12 Bidders will buy enough child REP at the repair auction

    Bidders are willing and able to supply the collateral a repair estimate requires at the offered terms, after discount, fees and capital costs. A full repair requires enough eligible fills to cover the shortfall.

    Failure boundary: Weak or strategically withheld demand leaves the child operational with impaired collateral. A shared security pool auction sells only the REP priced by its funded lots and can leave much of the shortfall unrepaired. Ordinary surplus withdrawal and retirement remain available to winners, so selling the inventory does not guarantee permanent backing.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: shared security pool auctions, and repair accounting.

  7. A13 REP is sufficiently liquid

    Participants can acquire or sell the required REP quantities without price impact or delay large enough to invalidate reporting, disputing, liquidation, auction, or migration economics.

    Failure boundary: Capital may exist in principle but cannot be assembled at a usable price before the deadline.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: economic roles, escalation, and oracle positions.

  8. A14 REP economic value exceeds the open interest it secures

    For a stated question ledger, lineage, or system scope at one timestamp, open interest and REP discounted cash flow are valued in the same numeraire, and REP discounted cash flow remains strictly greater. For a question-ledger-level evaluation, open interest means the question ledger’s protocol-accounted settlement collateral unless the analysis explicitly names a broader exposure measure. The REP supply and price basis must also be stated; market capitalization is the observable proxy in A08. This external relationship is separate from each question ledger’s onchain multiplier-adjusted REP backing check.

    Open Interest < Discounted Cash FlowREP

    Record scope, timestamp, common numeraire, price source, REP supply basis, and open-interest definition when evaluating this strict inequality.

    Failure boundary: REP holders may rationally accept or cause losses larger than the durable REP value placed at risk.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: security pool underwriting, liquidation backing, and oracle exposure analysis.

  9. A15 Question selection remains aligned with user intent

    Interfaces and users verify the immutable question ID and metadata when selecting a market or security pool, and the question has evidence and wording from which participants can reach an outcome or intentionally choose Invalid.

    Failure boundary: Unverified selection can route users and capital to an unintended market or security pool, while ambiguous wording or unavailable evidence can split local outcome coordination.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: UNI-02 and question identity.

Oracle correction, inclusion, and operational data

These assumptions cover truthful REP/collateral price discovery, deadline access, capital, independent correction, and the offchain information required to act. The shared feed uses the fixed report flags.

  1. A16 Deadline transactions can obtain Ethereum inclusion

    Honest users can submit competitively priced Ethereum transactions and obtain canonical inclusion within every relevant dispute, escalation, migration, auction, and settlement window.

    Selective exclusion can outlast a correction window even when gas is affordable. High base fees can also defer report acceptance under the settlement base-fee cap.

    Each onchain comparison assigns the exact deadline timestamp to one phase. Same-block ordering cannot move an action across that boundary: users must target the phase valid at the block timestamp rather than rely on transaction ordering to extend an expired window.

    Failure boundary: Censorship or congestion lasting through a deadline can finalize a wrong report or prevent a supported action.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: fork deadline guardrails, auction windows, and oracle censorship model.

  2. A17 OpenOracle has an available, adequately capitalized corrector

    For each harmful candidate report, a corrector can recognize the error, fund the replacement position in both assets and get the dispute included during its eligible window. Overlapping windows require enough free capital and execution capacity to cover concurrent reports. Different correctors may cover different windows.

    Failure boundary: An incorrect report can settle and authorize price-sensitive security pool actions when no capable participant detects, funds, and includes a correction.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: REP/collateral oracle, correction incentives, and attack model.

  3. A18 At least one available corrector has an independent net incentive

    At least one participant satisfying A17 is not under common control with the sponsor-funded shared-feed reporting path, does not share the manipulation payoff, and is not compensated to leave a harmful report uncorrected. The participant's net payoff from correcting is strictly greater than its net payoff from leaving the harmful report uncorrected. Two external submissions are not required for every request.

    The creation-time collateral floor must sustain that incentive even at zero aggregate exposure, after fees, gas, price impact and capital lockup.

    Failure boundary: An undersized floor, higher gas fees, or falling collateral value can leave a wrong REP/collateral price uneconomic to dispute. It may settle unchallenged and authorize unsafe price-sensitive actions even when every onchain funding check passes. A sponsor-controlled, bribed, or economically indifferent corrector can cause the same failure.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: settlement validation scope, economic tradeoffs, and oracle invariants.

  4. A19 A robust reference market makes harmful REP/collateral errors correctable

    For the actual pair and required size, independent participants can obtain an executable reference price from a market sufficiently independent of the price being checked. Every deviation large enough to affect the named pool action leaves a profitable correction under A18.

    Failure boundary: A harmful deviation below the profitable correction threshold, or a manipulated/illiquid reference market, can settle without an economically rational challenge.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: report sizing, external-payoff model, and settlement limits.

  5. A20 Canonical chain data and proof construction remain available

    Participants can obtain canonical history through the required snapshot and reconstruct the outcome root for an inherited claim. Standalone games also need a nullifier proof. Canonical games use a consumed-deposit check.

    Failure boundary: A valid dispute, settlement, migration, or inherited claim can become practically unavailable even while its contract entrypoint remains live.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: indexer replay and canonicality, carry proofs, and operator orientation.

Assets, clients, deployment, Ethereum, and cryptography

These assumptions define the technical foundation below the protocol’s onchain guards.

  1. A21 REP and selected collateral obey the required accounting model

    The accounting assumptions depend on the asset and the path using it.

    • Genesis REP. The theoretical supply is accurate, transfers deliver the requested 18-decimal units, and the burn sink remains inaccessible. Sepolia uses the reviewed genesis token once its deployment is verified under A23.
    • Child REP. Canonical children use the reviewed ReputationToken implementation.
    • Selected collateral. Transfers deliver exactly the raw units requested. The token supports the integration’s return-value and allowance conventions, including nonzero allowance changes where required. Rebases, fees, unexpected callbacks, blacklisting, pauses or unauthorized minting can break accounting or block delivery. Pools reject their deployment’s canonical REP as collateral.
    • WETH collateral. Holders can redeem WETH for ETH at parity. The Sepolia deployment checks the published WETH runtime hash.

    Failure boundary: Origin question ledger, genesis-fork, or oracle accounting can overstate balances, backing, burned supply, or collateral value.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: bounded lifecycle work and oracle token pair.

  2. A22 Participant-controlled recipients can accept required asset deliveries

    Recipients must be able to receive the asset transferred by their chosen path. Security pool fees, collateral redemptions, auction proceeds, and auction refunds use the selected ERC-20 collateral. Native-ETH router exits and OpenOracle ETH withdrawals or settler rewards require a recipient that accepts ETH. Contract recipients also return the required ERC-1155 receiver selector when complete-set creation, share migration, or an outcome-share transfer delivers tokens to them. Failed transfers or rejected callbacks can prevent delivery.

    Failure boundary: A failed collateral-token transfer, rejected native-ETH delivery, or rejected ERC-1155 callback can make the recipient’s payout, mint, migration, transfer, or claim unavailable even while protocol-wide accounting remains safe.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: router ETH callbacks, router ERC-1155 callbacks, share-token receipt, auction refunds, oracle request refunds, and security pool guardrails.

  3. A23 Users select verified canonical deployments

    The reviewed release is built reproducibly with its pinned compiler and toolchain configuration. The deterministic manifest freshness check rejects address drift, while the deployment plan verifies pinned runtime-code hashes for planned contracts and bootstrap descendants. The canonical factory deploys every security pool from pinned creation code and module deployers, and only those security pools can open a security pool namespace, so isSecurityPool on that factory authenticates a security pool and its modules. Users and integrations must still verify post-deployment wiring, factory and forker provenance, question ledger lineage, question ID, and the manifest against the intended network. Constructor admission alone is not a safety proof.

    Failure boundary: Toolchain drift, unavailable or mismatched reviewed hashes, permissionless lookalike instances, wrong dependencies, or unintended lineage and question wiring can bypass the reviewed protocol even when the selected bytecode functions as configured.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: release posture, deployment status, compiler configuration, OpenOracle build provenance, and authority invariants.

  4. A24 Clients, RPCs, and wallets preserve user intent and canonical state

    Interfaces, indexers, RPC providers, and wallet software return canonical, reorg-aware state; construct and display the intended chain, target, calldata, parameters, and ETH value; and submit only transactions the user authorizes. Users verify transaction previews rather than treating an application label as an onchain guarantee.

    Failure boundary: Compromised or stale offchain software can misroute signatures or capital, hide deadlines or events, or cause users to authorize valid but unintended calls without violating contract guards.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: deployment discovery, contract interactions.

  5. A25 Immutable parameters preserve economic and executable security margins

    The selected parameter relationships keep the fork threshold and fork haircut economically meaningful, the start bond affordable, the security pool backing multiplier (capital health) large enough for its coverage role, the oracle target error profitable to correct after fees and gas, dispute and settlement windows usable, and callback and transition gas executable. The security pool backing multiplier is independent of the fixed OraclePriceMath factors used to bound the report priority fee.

    Fork liveness also requires enough REP to have migrated into the active lineage. Thresholds use theoretical supply, so incomplete migration can make the fork backstop unreachable, even for holders of all circulating child REP.

    Failure boundary: Weak or internally inconsistent immutable parameters can defeat the economic or liveness argument even when the canonical contracts function exactly as configured.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: Statoblast security pool parameters, fork economics, liquidation economics, oracle parameters, and oracle attack model.

  6. A26 Ethereum preserves the execution environment the protocol relies on

    Ethereum continues to provide correct EVM execution and atomic rollback, sufficient canonical finality, usable timestamp and block.basefee semantics, and transaction and block gas limits and gas schedules under which every mandatory immutable transition remains executable.

    Failure boundary: Deep reorganizations, invalid state execution, adversarial time behavior, or gas repricing can reverse accepted state or strand required transitions.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: contract interactions, atomic external effects and router deadlines, and Escalation Game lifecycle.

  7. A27 Ethereum cryptographic primitives remain secure

    Keccak-256 remains collision- and preimage-resistant at the truncated widths used by the protocol, and CREATE2 identity remains sound. secp256k1 ECDSA signatures remain unforgeable with correct EOA signer recovery for Ethereum transactions and EIP-712 authorizations.

    Accounts using depositFromPermit2 or granting Permit2 allowances also rely on the fixed singleton, its signature checks, deadlines and nonce enforcement. Verify that implementation under A23. The standard shared-feed escrow starts reports with direct token approvals.

    Failure boundary: Forged identities, colliding questions or universes, false carry proofs, forged EOA transactions, incorrect EOA recovery or ERC-1271 acceptance, or broken domain and nonce enforcement can violate otherwise-correct guards or authorize an unintended token transfer.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: universe identity, proof hashing, Permit2 deposit authorization, Permit2 signature interface, EIP-712 authorization signer recovery, pinned Permit2 implementation provenance, and deterministic address guardrails.

  8. A28 Participants retain control of account authority

    Participants protect the private keys, signing devices, session permissions, and delegated ERC-20 or ERC-1155 approvals that authorize their accounts. Account recovery and custody arrangements do not give an unintended party effective control during a security-sensitive window.

    Failure boundary: A stolen key, compromised signer, or overbroad approval can authorize valid but unwanted transfers, deposits, bids, migrations, or configuration changes without violating a contract guard.

    Monitoring checks. Mechanics: transaction authority, security pool guardrails.

Guarantees deliberately not made

These outcomes remain possible without contradicting the security model. They should be included in user, operator, and integration risk disclosures.

  • EG01. Zoltar creates valid branches but does not enforce that the fork question is relevant to a user, establish the objectively truthful branch, obtain user consent to a fork, or force users to coordinate on one branch.
  • EG02. A Collateral Repair Auction may raise less than its repair target; the child then activates with migrated collateral plus accepted auction collateral.
  • EG03. Shared security pools bound ordinary commitment growth by the accepted report’s exposure limit and require a separate funded proof for additional growth. Settlement does not prove external price correctness.
  • EG04. Shared security pools isolate concurrent report escrows, but cannot guarantee that any particular disputed report settles within a bounded time. A participant with enough capital can keep correcting one candidate without blocking other independently funded candidates.
  • EG05. The immutable protocol has no administrator who can pause, upgrade, roll back, or rescue an unsafe deployment after launch.
  • EG06. Reporter escrow is separate from saleable auction inventory. A losing escalation claim pays its committed depositor nothing, and reporting claims remain subject to the game and fork rules; segregation does not guarantee a successful report or a particular REP value.
