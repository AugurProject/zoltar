Design explainer · pull request #1164

Shared security pools and uniform-price Truth Auctions

Pull request #1164 lets many Statoblast questions share one pool of REP security and one set of underwriting commitments, instead of each question running its own isolated SecurityPool. It also rebuilds the post-fork Truth Auction as a uniform-price auction of whole ETH lots whose winners automatically become funded capital providers. This page explains what the change does, how the new system works, the math and incentives behind it, and where its weak points are. It closes with the page author's assessment of the pull request and concrete suggestions for improving it.

1. The change in one minute

Statoblast is Augur's market layer on top of the Zoltar oracle. Before this pull request, every question had its own SecurityPool: its own vaults holding REP, its own ETH collateral, its own fee curve, its own REP/ETH price coordinator and, after a fork, its own Truth Auction. A REP holder who wanted to secure twenty questions had to run twenty vaults and pay for twenty price reports.

After this pull request, a portfolio holds REP once per universe in a capital ledger, and every question registered in the portfolio draws on that same backing. A provider deposits REP, picks a personal health target, and sets one ETH commitment. A buyer picks a question and an amount; the portfolio checks that total demand (ETH liabilities) stays within the healthy commitment of all providers, mints the complete set (one Invalid, one Yes and one No share per unit of ETH), and charges a fee made of a backing fee for providers and a prepaid resolution premium. Each question keeps its own outcome tokens, its own Escalation Game (the REP-staked dispute process that resolves a question locally) and separate settlement accounting, but ETH custody and fee bookkeeping are centralized in a settlement book.

The pull request also replaces per-vault liquidation with two actions: the owner's own cure (at a fresh price, lowering the commitment removes unbacked commitment at once, within unused commitment and never below the new value, for no reward) and a permissionless takeover (take over a distressed commitment for a time-ramped REP award), and redesigns the Truth Auction that repairs a branch's ETH shortfall after a fork: bids are whole ETH lots with a minimum REP-per-lot limit, every winning lot receives the same clearing REP amount, provably losing bids can be refunded before closing, in one transaction if the bidder wants, and all sold REP enters capital custody immediately so winners never need to claim. Each bid's vault is a cheap clone of one shared implementation, with the bidder, ledger, auction and bid number fixed in its own code.

No shared pot pays for upkeep. Whoever needs a fresh price pays for it, an owner pays for their own cure, and ETH that no current underwriter has earned and whose payer is not recorded, such as income from unhealthy or stale intervals and unused premiums, is burned rather than refunded, with one fork-time exception described in 5.2. Reporters are paid only under the Escalation Game's own REP rules; the portfolio adds no ETH reward.

Everything is built so that no operation iterates over all questions, owners or bidders: commitments live in balanced trees keyed by health, fees vest through cumulative integrals, and forks copy immutable roots instead of migrating each vault.

2. The problem: fragmented security

In the standalone design each SecurityPool is an island. A vault owner deposits REP into a specific pool and sets an underwriting commitment there. The pool can mint complete sets only up to the sum of its vault commitments, charges an open-interest fee that depends on that pool's utilization, and requires its own fresh REP/ETH price for any solvency-sensitive step. When the universe forks, each pool migrates its vaults one by one and each child pool runs its own Truth Auction, in which winners must come back later to claim a vault.

Three costs follow from that isolation:

  • Idle capital. REP locked in a quiet question cannot back a busy one. Underwriting capacity is wherever it happened to be deposited, not where demand is.
  • Repeated overhead. Every pool needs its own price reports, its own keeper attention and, in a fork, its own migration and auction. The costs scale with the number of questions, not with the amount of capital.
  • Thin markets. Small questions rarely attract a dedicated underwriter, so the long tail of questions is either unbacked or backed badly.

Standalone pools are not removed. A question can still be deployed as an ordinary SecurityPool with the existing vault, fee and auction rules. The portfolio is a second way to deploy and back questions, and the per-question SecurityPool contract now has a bound mode in which it mirrors the central books instead of holding assets itself.

3.2 The contracts

The portfolio is a hub with a small number of modules, each deployed once per portfolio or once per universe. The hub holds configuration and routes authenticated callbacks; the modules hold assets or accounting, never both for the same thing.

Three design rules recur in every module. First, custody and accounting are separate: the settlement book records liabilities, but the settlement contract holds the ETH; the commitment index records positions, but the capital ledger holds the REP. Second, every mutation is preceded by a checkpoint that pays out income earned at the old weights before any weight, price or commitment changes, so nobody can capture earnings that accrued before they joined. Third, work follows tree paths: no call loops over the set of owners, questions or bids.

4. How a portfolio works, step by step

This section follows a portfolio from creation to redemption. Numbers in the examples use illustrative parameters: a security multiplier of 150% (15,000 basis points), a 5% oracle tolerance, a 1% purchase fee, a 10% annual holding fee and a 1 ETH minimum commitment. All of these are configured per portfolio or per deployment, not fixed by the protocol, except where the text says otherwise.

4.1 Creating a portfolio and registering questions

A portfolio is created from a seed: an ordinary origin SecurityPool whose security multiplier and oracle priority fee the portfolio inherits. The creator supplies an immutable admission policy contract and an economics configuration. The repository ships two policies: Open, which admits any question, and Fixed, which admits a sorted list of question identifiers that can never change. Anyone can write another policy; it is consulted with a static call on registration, quotes and purchases, never on withdrawals or recovery, so a broken policy cannot trap funds.

Parameter Meaning Where it bites
purchaseFeeBps, minimumPurchaseFeeAttoEth One-time fee on each purchase, as a share of the backing value the purchase consumes Every purchase, including reused capacity
annualFeeBps Linear holding fee charged from a question's cash while it is open and unreported Continuous fee stream; also sizes the prepaid resolution premium
minimumCommitmentAttoEth Smallest commitment, smallest retirement step, and the ETH lot size of the Truth Auction Setting a commitment, retirement, cures, auction bids
rewardVestingSeconds How long each lump-sum fee budget (backing fee, reporting REP surplus) takes to vest to providers Backing-fee and REP-surplus credits
maximumQuestionHorizonSeconds Longest time to question end accepted at registration Registration
minimumOutcomeRate Minimum settlement units retained per outcome share after holding fees; also bounds new issuance Holding-fee budget and purchases

Setup runs in four permissionless stages, each one transaction, so that deploying the settlement book, reporter ledger, fork coordinator, capital ledger and shared price feed stays under the block gas limit. Registering a question afterwards deploys a thin bound SecurityPool and its ShareToken in the portfolio's own namespace, checks the question's end date against the horizon, and records it in the settlement book. Registration moves no capital and creates no per-provider state: a portfolio with ten thousand questions has exactly as many positions as it has providers.

4.2 Providing capital: principal, commitment, health

A provider interacts with one capital ledger per universe. Three quantities describe a provider there:

  • Principal is REP the provider has deposited. It is either free (idle, withdrawable at any time) or pledged to the provider's single live position.
  • Commitment is the ETH amount the provider agrees to underwrite. All pledged principal backs this one commitment; there is at most one live position per owner, portfolio and universe.
  • Health target is a personal multiplier, at least 100%, that the provider applies on top of the protocol floor. It governs admission, raising a commitment or setting it to its current value, and surplus withdrawals. The protocol applies only the floor, both for counting capacity and for liquidation; the target binds only the owner's own actions.

Health is measured against a REP/ETH price quoted as REP per ETH, so a higher price means REP is cheaper and more REP is needed. The REP a position must hold is:

requiredRep = commitment × price × securityMultiplier10 000 × healthTarget10 000

Each multiplication rounds up in the contract. The security multiplier is the seed pool's value, floored at 10,500 basis points (105%). The health target is at least 10,000 basis points.

Rather than re-evaluating every position at each new price, the index stores for every position the highest price at which it is still healthy at the protocol floor:

maxHealthyPrice = pledgedRep×10 000 securityMultiplier×commitment

Positions are keyed by this value in a balanced tree, so “sum of commitments healthy at price p” is one tree walk, whatever the number of providers.

Two prices are derived from each accepted oracle report, using the oracle's configured dispute tolerance (5% in the repository configuration):

conservativePrice = acceptedPrice1−tolerance liquidationPrice = acceptedPrice1+tolerance

Admission, commitment growth, surplus withdrawal and capacity use the conservative price (REP assumed cheaper than reported). Cures and takeovers use the liquidation price (REP assumed dearer than reported).

The gap between the two prices is the point of the design. A report that is wrong by up to the tolerance in either direction cannot both admit a position and then make it liquidatable: a position admitted at the edge needs the accepted price to move by a factor of (1 + tolerance) ÷ (1 − tolerance), about 10.5% at a 5% tolerance, before any keeper can touch it.

An owner sets their commitment with one call, setCommitment. Raising it, or setting it to its current value, requires a fresh price and enough pledged REP at the provider's own target and the conservative price; lowering it is a retirement (see 4.5). A raise also requires that the portfolio's aggregate exposure stays within what the accepted oracle report has funded (see 4.7); a provider who wants to grow beyond that can attach their own settled report with setCommitmentWithReport, which can raise or keep but never lower the commitment, and is then held to that report's conservative price as a withdrawal floor until a newer canonical report covers the larger total. Raising a live target is likewise price-checked; an atomic deposit-and-set-target call exists so a provider can add collateral and tighten their target in one step.

4.3 Buying complete sets: capacity and fees

A buyer calls the portfolio with a universe, a question, an ETH amount, a recipient and a minimum number of shares, sending the ETH plus a fee budget. Allocation is automatic: there is no choice of provider. The gating constraint is that new ETH liabilities are admitted only while the healthy commitment of all providers covers the total:

availableCapacity = healthyActiveCommitment ( conservativePrice ) − demand

demand is the ETH of all live question liabilities in the universe. healthyActiveCommitment counts active positions whose maxHealthyPrice is at least the conservative price, plus the part of a retiring position its owner has chosen to keep. The rule is enforced when demand increases and when retirements are served, not continuously: an adverse price move can leave outstanding demand above the healthy commitment, and it is the owners' cures and keepers' takeovers of 4.6, not a reduction of demand, that restore coverage. Capacity is zero while the price is stale, after the universe has forked, before a branch has been activated, and while the universe's settlement book is in runoff, the post-fork state in which claims settle but no new purchases are accepted (see 5.4).

Minting itself is proportional. A question's shares are a claim on its units of cash; a new buyer receives cash × outcomeSupply ÷ units complete sets, so holders who arrived before fees were charged are not diluted and later buyers pay the current exchange rate. Every mint increases demand by exactly the cash deposited.

The fee has two parts. The first is proportional to the backing value the purchase consumes, which is the cash times the security multiplier, because that is the amount of REP exposure providers take on:

backingFee = max ( minimumPurchaseFee , cash × securityMultiplier10 000 × purchaseFeeBps10 000 )

The second prepays the holding fee for the reporting tail. Once a question's Escalation Game opens, the linear holding fee stops, but providers remain exposed until finality. The buyer therefore prepays a resolution premium covering the longest possible local reporting window, three days of activation plus seven weeks of required-support ramp (52 days):

premium = addedPrincipal × annualFeeBps10 000 × 52 days365 days

addedPrincipal is the cash for a fresh question; on a question with shares it is the original cash behind the new shares, which holding fees do not reduce. The premium is earned by providers only until the question's finality deadline; the unused remainder is burned, because the premium book does not record which buyer paid it. The one exception is the fork-time cash recovery of 5.2, which returns the matching premium to whoever holds the complete sets.

The whole backing fee vests to providers, and the premium is the only other charge:

totalFee=backingFee+premium providerIncome=totalFee−premium=backingFee

Until 340d802f1 a third part topped up a per-question reporting budget (see the glossary); it is gone. Quotes are exact at the current checkpoint. On a question that already has shares, holding fees lower the question's units but not the original cash behind them, so the same ETH buys slightly more shares and a slightly larger premium each block, and a quote can be too low a few blocks later. The transaction re-checkpoints, enforces the caller's total budget and minimum shares, and refunds any excess ETH; the SDK allows a fee up to 1% above the quote.

Worked example: the first 1 ETH purchase of a fresh question.

4.4 Earning: holding fees and vesting streams

Providers earn from four sources: the up-front backing fee, the resolution premium, a linear holding fee charged continuously from each open question's cash, and the REP surplus that Escalation Games shed (4.8). The holding fee replaces the standalone pools' utilization-dependent retention curve with something a buyer can compute in advance:

feeRatePerSecond = feePrincipal×annualFeeBps 10 000×365 days

feePrincipal is the cash originally deposited for the question's live shares, so the fee is a flat annual percentage of what buyers paid. It stops when an Escalation Game opens, when the question is final, before another whole second of fees would cross the question's minimum backing floor, or while the universe's price is stale or its book is in runoff. Each unit charged moves question cash into fee cash and lowers demand by the same amount.

The backing floor is the outstanding outcome-share supply multiplied by minimumOutcomeRate, divided by 1018 and rounded up to a whole settlement unit. Only units above it may fund holding fees. With the UI default of 0.01, 1,000 complete sets retain at least 10 ETH in a fully funded book. Each stream's expiry is capped by that fee budget, so aggregate checkpoints enforce the floor without visiting questions or requiring keeper transactions. Purchases and redemptions remain proportional at the floor; direct fee deductions cannot spend the reserve either.

The cap ends ordinary holding-fee income without releasing the remaining underwriting obligation. Providers must price that possible period into their decision to back a portfolio; purchase fees and prepaid resolution premiums remain separate. The floor is not a guaranteed ETH recovery value after an underfunded fork, and it cannot eliminate rounding below a token's smallest unit.

Income reaches providers in two different ways. Time-earned income, the holding fee and the resolution premium, is credited at every checkpoint to the commitments that are healthy at that moment, in proportion to size. Lump-sum income, each backing fee and the REP surplus from reporting, is handed to a fee stream contract that vests it linearly over rewardVestingSeconds. Vesting serves two purposes: it rewards capital that stays, and it prevents a provider who joins just before a large lump sum is credited from capturing income that belongs to earlier providers. The stream contract keeps one cumulative integral per expiry time, so adding a budget or releasing income costs a constant amount of work plus a binary search over outstanding expiries, no matter how many budgets were ever funded:

incomeReleased (from,to) = ∑stream ratestream × ( min(to,endstream) − min(from,endstream) )

Each stream's rate is its integer budget divided by the vesting duration; the integer remainder is paid at the stream's end. Later funding never accelerates an existing stream.

Whether it arrives from a stream or from a checkpoint, income is split across commitments in proportion to size, but only healthy commitments receive their share. Unhealthy commitments stay in the denominator, so their part is not redistributed to others; ETH and REP that cannot be assigned are burned, in every state of the universe. Income that accrues while the price is stale is unassignable in the same way. Positions carry their earned ETH and REP separately from principal, and a provider claims earnings in one call.

4.5 Exiting: surplus, retirement and the queue

Free principal can be withdrawn at any time, including after a fork. Pledged principal above the requirement at the owner's target and the conservative price is surplus and can be withdrawn whenever the price is fresh and the universe is open:

withdrawable = freeRep + max ( 0 , pledgedRep − requiredRep (commitment,healthTarget,ownerConservativePrice) )

Reducing the commitment itself is a retirement. A provider sets a lower commitment (zero for a full exit; otherwise both the new value and the reduction must meet the minimum). At a fresh price an underwater position is first cured at once, never below the new value (4.6); whatever reduction remains moves the position out of the active tree into a cohort, the queue of everyone who asked in the same period. At a stale price nothing is cured and the whole reduction is queued. Retirement is served only from spare capacity:

spare = healthyCommitment (conservativePrice) − demand

Service happens lazily, whenever something else runs a checkpoint: deposits, commitment changes, purchases, complete-set returns and takeovers all try to serve the queue first. Returning complete sets reduces demand immediately, so redemptions free capacity for exits in the same transaction.

The queue follows a few fixed rules:

  • Earlier cohorts are served before later ones.
  • Within a cohort, partial service starts from the healthiest positions; a cohort's unhealthy members leave together with it once spare capacity covers the cohort's healthy commitment.
  • A single position can be served on request only if spare capacity also covers every healthy reduction queued ahead of it, and that targeted service is all-or-nothing.
  • A pending request is locked until it is served: it cannot be cancelled or changed, and any other value reverts with “Retirement pending”. Repeating the pending value leaves the request where it is and, at a fresh price, cures an underwater position in place.
  • Raising the commitment, or keeping its current value, is possible only for a position with no pending request, and requires a fresh price and the owner's target.
  • A provider cannot bypass the queue: a lower value queues every part of the reduction except the part the owner's cure removes at once.

The second rule follows from the capacity accounting rather than from a preference: only healthy commitment occupies the capacity an exit releases, so unhealthy commitment can leave without waiting for spare capacity of its own. The same reasoning lets an owner's cure skip the queue: it removes only commitment that never counted as capacity, so it takes nothing from earlier requests.

4.6 Distress: cures and takeovers instead of liquidation

Standalone pools liquidate by moving commitment and REP to an approved receiver vault with a 5% REP bonus and a staged price request. Portfolios replace this with the owner's own cure and a permissionless takeover, neither of which needs an approval registry, staging or an ETH reserve.

Distress is observed, not declared. Every accepted price records which positions are healthy at the liquidation price. A position's distress clock starts at the first adverse observation after its most recent recovery, and no keeper transaction can start, reset or extend it. A deposit at a stale price does not clear distress; only a healthy mutation at a fresh price does.

Cure

A cure removes only as much of a distressed position's commitment as is unused by the portfolio, that is, total commitment minus demand, and, subject to the minimum rules below, about as much as needed to make the position healthy again at the liquidation price. Only the owner can send one, by lowering the commitment with setCommitment at a fresh price (or, with a pending request, by repeating its value); the cure then never cuts below the new value, and it pays nobody. A takeover also cures its target first, as if the new value were zero:

supportedCommitment=pledgedRep×10 000securityMultiplier×liquidationPrice removed=min(commitment−newCommitment,unusedCommitment,max(minimumCommitment,commitment−supportedCommitment))

unusedCommitment is total commitment minus demand, counting every position whether healthy or not, so it is at least the spare capacity of 4.5. supportedCommitment rounds down, so the deficit rounds up and removing it leaves a remainder that the position's REP fully backs. A cure that could remove less than one minimum commitment without removing the whole position removes nothing. The position keeps all of its REP; if unused commitment, the owner's requested target or the minimum-size rules limit removal below the deficit, the remaining position stays distressed. If the position is already healthy or nothing can be removed, nothing is cured; for an owner's lowering, the whole reduction is then queued. Any reduction left between the cure and a nonzero new value must still be a valid retirement request: if it would be smaller than one minimum commitment, the cure removes it too when unused commitment allows, and otherwise removes less so that the queued rest is exactly one minimum commitment, or, if that would leave less than one minimum to remove, removes nothing and queues the whole reduction.

Worked example: a provider pledged 3,000 REP against a 10 ETH commitment at 150%. The accepted price rises to 230 REP per ETH, so the liquidation price is 219.05. Supported commitment is 3,000 ÷ 1.5 ÷ 219.05 = 9.13 ETH, a deficit of 0.87 ETH, which the minimum commitment rounds up to a 1 ETH removal (if the portfolio has at least 1 ETH of unused commitment). If the provider sets the commitment to 9 ETH, that 1 ETH is removed at once rather than queued. The provider keeps a 9 ETH commitment backed by all 3,000 REP, against the 2,957 REP that 9 ETH requires at the liquidation price, so the position is healthy again. If they set it to zero instead, the cure still removes 1 ETH at once and the other 9 ETH wait in the retirement queue. Either way the provider paid the gas and nobody was rewarded.

Takeover

If the owner does not cure, or a cure cannot restore health because unused commitment does not cover the deficit or the position is insolvent, a keeper can take over part or all of its commitment. The takeover first performs any possible cure, unpaid as always, then the keeper receives the position's REP proportional to the commitment taken, scaled by how long the position has been in distress:

award = pledgedRep × commitmentTakencommitment × min ( 1 , now−distressStart1 hour )

The receiver's existing position, the award and all of the receiver's idle REP together must back the combined commitment at the receiver's own health target and the conservative price; the receiver states the most idle REP they consent to sweeping in, and the call reverts if their idle balance exceeds it. REP not awarded stays with the source owner: in a partial takeover it remains pledged behind the reduced commitment, and in a full takeover everything above the award is returned to the source owner as free principal.

Takeovers have a bounded variant for bots. A bounded takeover pins the price epoch, the receiver's own health target and the source cohort, caps the receiver's resulting principal and commitment, and demands a minimum award. The transaction reverts if any pinned value changed. Auction winners cure their own positions through their vault like any owner, and their positions can be taken over; the aggregate auction position is first split so that one winner's loss is never charged to other bidders.

4.7 One price per universe: the shared feed

Every portfolio universe has one shared price feed that replaces the per-pool price coordinators for solvency purposes. Anyone can sponsor a report: they fund the OpenOracle game (the contestable price-report mechanism Statoblast uses for REP/ETH prices) with WETH and REP from their own wallet, and the feed accepts the settled result if it is fresh, newer than the last one, economically defensible at the current base fee, and sized for the portfolio's exposure. Nothing reimburses a sponsor: whoever needs a fresh price, typically a provider who wants to withdraw or grow, a buyer who needs capacity or a keeper who wants to take over, pays for the report. Before this revision the feed called back into the portfolio on settlement to pay accepted sponsors from a maintenance reserve, at most once per 480 seconds per universe and up to a gas-based cap; the callback and the reserve are gone.

Report sizing is where price security meets capital size. A report's WETH side must cover one hundredth of the aggregate exposure, and its REP side one hundredth of any auction inventory, so a report cannot authorize more risk than it would cost to correct it:

exposure=max(totalCommitment,auctionExposure,demand) acceptedExposureLimit=(reportWeth−gasFloor)×100

Commitment growth is allowed only while exposure stays within the accepted report's limit. An owner who wants to grow past it attaches their own settled, larger report through setCommitmentWithReport; the ledger then keeps that report's conservative price as that owner's withdrawal floor until a later canonical report covers the enlarged total. An older, smaller report can never authorize larger risk.

Reports are independent candidates with separate escrows, so a disputed report cannot block a different sponsor from establishing a fresh observation, and a pending report never extends the accepted price's expiry. Accepted prices are valid for the oracle's settlement time plus five minutes on mainnet (one hour on Sepolia), which is why almost every solvency-sensitive action checks price fresh and why the ledger pauses cash-funded fees across stale intervals.

4.8 Reporting, finality and redemption

Each question still resolves through its own Escalation Game: REP-backed balances accumulate behind Invalid, Yes and No after the question ends, the median balance sets the deadline, and a strict leader at the deadline wins locally. What changes is custody and where the surplus goes. Reporter REP for all questions in a universe sits in one reporter ledger whose per-question roots are copied to fork children without traversal. The irrevocable surplus that the game sheds whenever the smallest outcome balance rises is routed to providers as vested REP income rather than to a per-pool backing pot.

The portfolio pays reporters nothing of its own. Until 340d802f1 it funded a capped ETH reporting reward for the final reporter (see the glossary); that revision removed the reward, its reward shares in the settlement book, the reporter callback from the game and the claim transaction, so a reporter's only return is the Escalation Game's REP payout. 6.5 covers the free-rider problem that remains.

When a question's deadline passes, the settlement book freezes its units and their pro-rata share of cash (and any recovery REP, the child REP owed to claims after a fork, see 5.4) into a frozen book, so later fee accrual and later fork shortfalls cannot touch finalized claims. Winning shares and complete sets redeem against the central cash; a redemption burns the shares through the question's ShareToken, lowers demand, cancels any unused premium when a question has no live shares left, and lets the ledger serve the retirement queue in the same transaction.

5. Forks and the Truth Auction

A fork is Zoltar's last resort: when an Escalation Game cannot decide, the universe splits into one child per outcome and every REP holder picks the child they believe is true. Statoblast must then move each question's security into the children. In a standalone pool each vault owner migrates per pool and each child pool auctions REP to repair its missing ETH. The portfolio does this once per universe.

5.2 Freeze, choose, migrate

When the universe forks, the fork coordinator freezes the universe's capital ledger, settlement book, premium book and reporter ledger. The freeze checkpoints every fee earned before the fork and records five totals: pledged REP backing, total commitment, question cash, question units and the live recovery REP owed to claims. During the eight-week choice window each owner with pledged principal calls choose once, naming one outcome. The owner's exact position, including its health target, retirement request and remaining-commitment goal, is exported from the parent ledger and imported into the chosen child's ledger, which is still in import mode and accepts only fork imports until the branch starts, then only price reports until it activates.

Cash follows commitment, not REP. A branch receives the share of the parent's question cash that its choosers had committed to underwrite:

branchCash = parentCash × chosenCommitmentparentCommitment shortfall = parentCash − branchCash

Excess personal collateral cannot pull more ETH into a branch than its owners committed to underwrite. The premium book splits by the same ratio; premium that elapses while a child is being prepared is burned. After the choice deadline a holder of a live question's complete sets can recover the unchosen share of its cash from the parent, and the matching share of its remaining premium comes back with it: this is the one path on which unused premium returns to a share holder instead of being burned. Each branch inherits all of the parent's question liabilities, so the shortfall is exactly the cash the non-choosing owners were underwriting.

After the choice deadline anyone can start a branch. Zoltar migrates the frozen backing REP plus live recovery REP into the child as child REP; the branch's chosen backing goes to its capital ledger, and the frozen backing of owners who chose another branch or did not choose at all becomes the branch's fresh inventory. If the branch has outstanding question units, fresh inventory and a shortfall of at least one minimum commitment, the coordinator deploys a branch Truth Auction and hands it the inventory. Older recovery REP from previous forks never enters the auction; it stays with the claims that already own it.

Missing the cutoff is costly. An owner who does not choose within eight weeks loses their pledged principal's branch fallback: that REP is sold in every branch's auction to repair the cash their commitment was backing. Their idle principal and already-earned fees remain separately claimable from the parent ledger.

5.3 The Truth Auction: uniform-price lots

The auction sells fresh inventory REP for ETH to repair the shortfall, and nothing else. It does not select truth: it sells REP of a child universe that already exists. Its design differs from the standalone tick auction in almost every detail, so it is worth stating the whole mechanism.

Units

The ETH side is denominated in whole lots, each equal to the portfolio's minimum commitment. The REP side is denominated in a grid that makes the per-lot collateral divide exactly for any number of lots:

lotEth=minimumCommitment targetLots=⌊shortfall÷lotEth⌋ repGrid=securityMultiplier÷gcd(securityMultiplier,10 000)

At a 150% multiplier the grid is 3 attoREP, so grid rounding is invisible in practice. A shortfall below one lot cannot be repaired by the auction.

Opening

The auction opens only against a funded, fresh shared price whose WETH side covers the branch's maximum possible exposure (chosen commitment plus every target lot) and whose REP side covers the inventory. The opening conservative price fixes the minimum collateral per lot for the whole auction, rounded up to the grid, and the inventory must be able to back at least one lot:

minimumRepPerLot (healthTarget) = requiredRep (lotEth,openingConservativePrice,securityMultiplier,healthTarget)

Bidding

A bid is a triple: a number of lots, a minimum REP per lot the bidder insists on receiving (a grid multiple), and a health target for the vault the bid will become. The bidder pays exactly lots × lotEth in ETH and nothing else: no REP deposit, no token approval, no execution reserve. The minimum REP per lot must at least cover the lot at the bidder's own health target at the opening price, so a winning bid is always a healthy vault on arrival. Bids cannot be cancelled or repriced. Each bid also creates a small auction vault controlled by the bidder that will own the resulting position. The vault is a minimal-proxy clone (EIP-1167) of one shared implementation, published once by the auction deployer rather than by the fork coordinator; the bidder's address, the capital ledger, the auction and the bid number are appended to the clone's own code and read back on every call. Creating it costs less than a fifth of a full vault deployment, the bindings can never change, and the address follows from those bindings and the shared implementation. The implementation itself refuses to be used directly.

A lower minimum REP per lot is a higher price: the bidder accepts fewer REP for the same ETH. Bids rank by that minimum, lowest first, ties by arrival.

Clearing

At closing the engine finds the largest prefix of ranked lots that the inventory can fund at the marginal bidder's own minimum, capped by the target. Equal-price bids at the margin share the last lots by cumulative proportional whole-lot rounding. Then one of two cases applies:

filledLots=max{lotCount:lotCount≤min(targetLots,⌊inventory÷minimumRepPerLot(bid holding lot lotCount)⌋)} target reached: repPerLot=minimumRepPerLot(marginal bid),soldRep=filledLots×repPerLot target missed: repPerLot=⌊inventory÷filledLots⌋ on the grid,soldRep=inventory

Every winning lot receives the same REP amount. When the target is reached, unsold inventory returns to the branch as recovery REP for existing claims. When it is missed, all inventory is sold, so winners receive at least their minimum and usually more; the indivisible grid remainder is credited to winners as REP earnings in proportion to their lots.

Closing is permissionless at the week-nine deadline and is never extended. The quote is tested at the deadline, not at the time of the call, so a late closer cannot pick a better price. Fills are cancelled and all ETH refunded if the branch universe forked again before closing, if no funded fresh quote covers the deadline, or if the closing conservative price is worse for the branch than the opening one, meaning REP got cheaper and the pledged collateral would no longer back the commitments. Cancellation does not block branch activation; the branch simply activates with the migrated cash alone.

Two worked examples

Four bidders face a 10 ETH shortfall with 1 ETH lots at a 150% multiplier and an opening conservative price of 200 REP per ETH, so one lot needs at least 300 REP at a 100% health target.

Bidder (rank) Lots Minimum REP per lot Health target Example A: 3,000 REP inventory Example B: 6,000 REP inventory
Alice (1) 4 320 100% 4 lots, 1,714.3 REP 4 lots, 1,600 REP
Bob (2) 3 350 110% 3 lots, 1,285.7 REP 3 lots, 1,200 REP
Carol (3) 5 400 120% 0 lots, 5 ETH refundable early 3 lots, 1,200 REP, 2 ETH refunded
Dave (4) 2 500 150% 0 lots, 2 ETH refundable early 0 lots, 2 ETH refundable early
Result 7 of 10 lots; all 3,000 REP sold at 428.57 per lot (an 18 attoREP grid remainder goes to winners as REP earnings); 7 ETH raised, branch in runoff 10 of 10 lots; 4,000 REP sold at 400 per lot; ETH shortfall fully repaired, but the 2,000 unsold REP becomes recovery REP for existing claims, so the branch still settles in runoff

In example A the inventory cannot fund an eighth lot at Carol's minimum (3,000 ÷ 400 = 7 lots, and seven better-ranked lots already exist), so the auction stops at seven lots and sells everything it has: each winner receives about 428.57 REP per lot, more than they asked for. In example B the target binds, so the marginal bidder Carol sets the uniform price and Alice, who would have accepted 320 REP, receives 400 REP per lot like everyone else.

Early refunds for provably losing bids

Because bids cannot be cancelled or repriced, demand that ranks ahead of a bid can only grow. A bid is therefore permanently excluded as soon as strictly better funded demand alone would fill the auction at that bid's price:

betterLots ≥ min ( targetLots , ⌊inventory÷minimumRepPerLot⌋ )

betterLots counts lots with a strictly lower minimum. Anyone may credit the refund of a qualifying bid before closing; only the bidder may withdraw it, to a recipient of their choice, and may use the ETH anywhere, including a new bid at a better price. The bidder can also credit their own bid and withdraw in one transaction; that call skips a credit someone else already made and pays out the bidder's entire refund credit in the auction, including refunds already credited for their other bids. A new bid that already satisfies this condition is rejected outright. A bid with a provisional zero fill from equal-price rounding is not refundable early, because later equal bids can still change the rounding.

Refunded bids are not deleted. They remain in the auction's balanced tree as permanently zero-fill history, so clearing and fee ownership never need a scan or a deletion, at the cost of storage that grows with every bid ever placed.

5.4 After closing: winners are owners

At finalization the raised ETH goes to the branch's settlement book and all sold REP goes to the child's capital ledger as one protected aggregate position owned by the auction contract. That position accounts for every winner's commitment, fees and later fork exposure. The branch activates immediately; no winner has to claim anything to be counted as backing.

Any owner or keeper can later split out one bid from the aggregate into its own position, carrying its exact REP, commitment, health target, earned fees and distress history. The split is proportional and exact (the grid guarantees it), and it does not grant a new distress clock. Nobody can take over the aggregate as a whole, and only a split-out position can be cured, so one winner's loss is never charged to other bidders. The bidder's auction vault contract controls the position: it can deposit more REP, set a target, set its commitment (which includes retiring and curing), claim earnings and make fork choices like any other owner, and sweep returned assets to a wallet in the same call. Before its first owner operation the vault splits out its own position if the bid won any lot, then records a permanent split-out flag so later owner operations skip the auction lookup and the ledger call; if that first operation reverts, the split and the flag roll back together. The flag covers only the capital ledger the bid was sold into; if that universe later forks, the vault's positions in the child ledgers keep their own checks. A losing bid's vault never records the flag and stays usable for recovering assets.

The branch then lives in one of two states. If accepted ETH plus migrated cash covers all inherited units and no recovery REP remains, which requires the auction to have sold its entire inventory, it is fully operational: purchases reopen and holding fees resume. Otherwise it is in runoff: existing claims settle pro rata in ETH and in recovery REP (unsold inventory plus older recovery baskets, tracked with 128-bit precision so partial redemptions and nested forks keep exact ownership), but no new purchases are accepted and no holding fees are charged. New buyers can never capture an old recovery basket.

6. Game theory and incentives

This section asks, for each participant, why they would behave as the design wants, and where the design admits it cannot make them.

6.1 Why REP backing matters at all

Complete sets are fully collateralized by the buyer's own ETH, so in normal operation providers' REP is never used to pay anyone. Its job is the fork. When the universe splits, the ETH a branch receives is proportional to the commitment of the owners who chose it, and the REP of everyone who chose against it is sold in that branch to make its share holders whole. Because every healthy position holds at least securityMultiplier times its commitment in REP value, the inventory a branch can sell is worth, at the pre-fork price, at least the multiplier times the cash it is missing, as long as those positions were healthy at the freeze. If the branch's REP keeps its value, full repair is affordable; if the branch is the one the market considers false, its REP is worth little and the shortfall persists, which is the intended outcome. Providers are thus paid fees to stand behind their belief about which branch is true, and the admission rule that new demand must fit within healthy commitment keeps that promise collateralized at the moment each liability is taken on; between price reports, owners' cures and keepers' takeovers are what pull a drifting position back under cover.

6.2 Providers: fees for risk, with a dial

A provider's income is proportional to commitment while healthy, so the dominant strategy is to commit as much as their REP supports. The counterweight is the health target. A target of exactly 100% maximizes commitment per REP but leaves the position one adverse report away from distress, where the position stops earning and a takeover award starts to ramp unless the owner cures it by lowering the commitment. A higher target costs capital efficiency and buys distance from that zone. Because the target also gates surplus withdrawal, a provider who sets it high cannot quietly drain collateral later without first lowering it, and the withdrawal itself is then price-checked against the new target. The design leaves this trade-off to each owner rather than imposing a pool-wide ratio, so a mix of conservative and aggressive providers can coexist in one ledger without one subsidizing the other's liquidation.

Vesting is the anti-sniping device for lump sums. Without it, a provider could join seconds before a backing fee is credited, take a proportional share and leave. With vesting, joining late only earns the slice of each stream that vests after joining. Time-earned income needs no vesting: each checkpoint pays only the interval since the last one, to the commitments healthy during it, and the checkpoint-before-mutation rule guarantees that income already earned at the old weights is distributed at those weights.

6.3 The two-price band as a griefing defence

The oracle is contestable, not infallible: a report within the dispute tolerance is accepted. If one price were used for both admission and liquidation, a reporter could post a price at the top of the tolerance, watch providers be admitted at the edge, then post one at the bottom and liquidate them, all within “acceptable” reports. Using the conservative price for admission and the liquidation price for punishment means an accepted report can never do both: the minimum move between the two is (1 + tolerance) ÷ (1 − tolerance). Distress itself is declared by the oracle's observations, not by keepers. An owner can cure as soon as a fresh price leaves their position unhealthy, and the takeover award ramps over an hour from that observation, so before the full REP behind a commitment is at stake the owner has a window that no keeper can shorten.

6.4 Keepers: paid in REP for takeovers; cures belong to owners

A cure pays nobody, and since c07ee07e5 only the owner can send one, by lowering their own commitment; a takeover also cures first. A cure removes unused commitment, so it costs the provider nothing, and it is the owner who needs it: when the cure removes the position's whole deficit (subject to the minimum rules in 4.6), the cured remainder is healthy again, earns fees again and is no longer distressed, so no takeover can follow on that observation. Until c07ee07e5 anyone could cure, so another provider, a buyer or a retiring owner could cut someone else's position; until aa5f8efce a keeper also earned 1% of the backing value removed, in REP from the cured position. Owner-only cures end any hunting of marginal positions, and nobody else can shrink a commitment without an adverse observation. The liquidator bot cannot send a standalone cure; it cures only inside a takeover, which runs the cure first. The takeover award can be large, up to all the REP behind the taken commitment, but it starts at zero and takes an hour to ramp, and the keeper must usually bring REP of their own, because the award alone was by definition not enough to back the commitment, so taking over is a capital decision rather than a free option. There is no ETH liquidation bonus and no ETH execution reserve: keepers are paid in REP they must value and, if they want ETH, sell themselves. The liquidator bot shipped with the pull request values REP awards conservatively, predicts the unpaid cure a takeover performs first, rejects every takeover candidate unless independent REP price evidence agrees, and ships with portfolio exposure limits that default to zero.

6.5 Reporters: REP stakes, no ETH reward

Reporting pays only through the Escalation Game: the winning side's REP payout under the game's own rules, after the escalation haircut. Earlier revisions added a per-question ETH reporting reward, paid only to the reporter who created a new unique leader (a self top-up earned nothing) and capped at min(startBond, leadMargin) ÷ 5 (a colluding lead flip could not farm it); 340d802f1 removed the reward, and with it the attribution rule, the cap and the burn of unclaimed budgets. What remains is a free-rider question rather than a farming one: every share holder and every provider with occupied capacity gains from a report, so each can wait for someone else to stake REP and pay gas, and a small or abandoned question may stay open until a holder decides the redemption is worth the cost.

6.6 Bidders: uniform price, no promise of truthfulness

In a uniform-price auction every winner pays the marginal price, which weakens the incentive to shade bids compared with pay-as-bid, but it does not remove it: a bidder with a large share of demand still gains from demanding more REP per lot (bidding a lower price) if that lowers the clearing level for all their lots. The pull request states this plainly: uniform pricing does not guarantee competitive or truthful bidding. The lot-and-grid design is chosen for a different reason, which is that winning bids become healthy vaults with no further action, so the auction cannot leave REP in limbo waiting for claims.

The weak-demand rule is the most consequential choice. When the target is missed, all inventory is sold to whoever showed up, at inventory ÷ filledLots per lot. That maximizes repair and avoids a seller reserve that would leave REP unsold, but it means a lone bidder for one lot can buy the entire inventory for one minimum commitment of ETH. Winners then have ordinary vault obligations and no minimum holding period: with spare capacity they can retire and withdraw surplus REP immediately. Selling every REP therefore does not guarantee that every REP stays pledged; the enforceable backing is the accepted commitment at the chosen health target, no more.

Early refunds make bidding cheaper for losers and let ETH recycle into better bids, but they rely on the fact that excluded bids can never become winning demand again; that is why refunded bids stay in the tree as zero-fill history rather than being removed.

6.7 Choosers: one branch, one position, no hedging with pledged REP

A fork is a vote with capital. Each owner's live principal and commitment go to exactly one child. Idle principal is left in the parent and can be withdrawn and used anywhere, so an owner who wants to hedge must do it with free REP, not with the REP that is earning fees. Cash following commitment rather than REP removes a subtler game: an owner who over-collateralized could otherwise drag more than their share of ETH into a branch by holding excess REP. Not choosing is strictly dominated, because the pledged REP is auctioned in every branch for the benefit of share holders, while choosing any branch at least preserves it there.

6.8 The retirement queue

Exits compete with new purchases for the same spare capacity. Serving the queue before purchases, in cohort order, and refusing targeted service that would skip a healthy earlier request, means a provider cannot be trapped behind a stream of new buyers and cannot buy their way to the front. An owner's cure may skip the queue only because it removes unbacked commitment that earlier requests were never waiting for, and a pending request is locked until served, so a provider cannot hold an early slot while changing what they asked for.

7. Why it scales: trees instead of scans

A single ledger serving thousands of questions and providers is only viable if no transaction's cost depends on how many of them exist. The pull request replaces every scan with a tree path or a cumulative index.

Current interfaces and implementation details

Validation results belong to the revision on which they ran; historical test counts do not establish that the current head or its CI passes.

  • One commitment target. Owners use setCommitment; the report variant can raise or retain the value but cannot lower it. Lowering at a fresh price attempts an unpaid cure before queuing the remaining reduction. A pending retirement is locked until served; while it remains pending, only repeating its requested value is allowed. There is no standalone third-party cure or cancellation call.
  • No shared maintenance budget and no reporting reward. Price sponsors receive no service reimbursement. Unassigned fee income and unused resolution premiums are burned, apart from the premium a holder recovers with unchosen cash after a fork. Since 340d802f1 a purchase pays two charges only: the backing fee, which vests entirely to eligible providers, and the resolution premium.
  • Vault clones. Every bid creates a minimal-proxy vault bound to its bidder, ledger, auction and bid number. Verifiers check the shared implementation against the compiled artifact. Look-alike clones are ordinary owners with no auction authority, so they cannot stall the bot's position scan.
  • One-step refunds. A bidder can credit an eligible bid and withdraw their whole refund credit in one transaction, including when someone already credited the bid. Eligibility means provably losing before closing or having unfilled lots afterwards. The UI discloses the total before signing.
  • Cached winner materialization. A vault records a successful, nonzero initial position once, so later owner operations on that ledger skip the auction lookup. A reverting operation rolls the flag back; an unfilled bid does not set it.
  • Bounded reads and measured gas. Index claims return live commitment, zero unassigned income skips work, and services reuse the ledger read within a checkpoint. Lifecycle tests compare 8 and 256 tied bids, require growth below 350,000 gas on the measured paths, and impose a 3,000,000-gas ceiling except on the three child-module deployment transactions. These are tested bounds, not a universal constant-gas guarantee.
  • Walletless preview. simScenario=portfolio-auction seeds a forked auction. Scenarios can open from prepared snapshots when the build carries a current one, avoiding a replay of their seeding.
  • Dependency pins and render cost. e4ce60108 raises follow-redirects and adds root overrides for glob and minimatch to clear the audit advisories, and builds the UI's deployment bytecode catalogue once per mount instead of on every render.

9. Benefits

  • Capital goes where demand is. One deposit supplies shared capacity for purchases across registered questions; a quiet question no longer strands REP, and a busy one no longer waits for a dedicated underwriter.
  • Costs scale with capital, not with questions. One price report, one keeper watch and one fork migration per universe serve any number of questions. Registering a question moves no capital and creates no per-provider state.
  • Predictable prices for buyers. A flat annual holding fee and a purchase fee proportional to consumed backing replace a utilization-dependent retention curve; quotes bound both the total payment and the shares delivered.
  • Individual, not pooled, risk. Providers keep exact ownership of their principal, choose their own health target, and bear only their own cure and takeover losses. Unassigned income is never redistributed to whoever happens to be healthy.
  • Keeper actions without trusted setup. Takeovers need no approval registry, staging, ETH reserve or bonus pool, and bots can sign exact takeover bounds so a changed state cannot be exploited.
  • Owners control their own commitment. One call raises, keeps or lowers it, and lowering also cures. Nobody else can shrink a position except through a takeover, which needs an adverse observation.
  • No upkeep budget to drain or misallocate. With the maintenance reserve, the cure reward and the reporting reward gone, no shared pot can be farmed, run dry or steered by whoever posts reports; fee ETH that no underwriter or share holder can claim is burned.
  • Griefing-resistant pricing. The conservative and liquidation prices prevent a report within tolerance from both admitting and liquidating a position; independent report candidates prevent one disputed report from stalling the feed.
  • Auction winners are backing on arrival. All sold REP enters custody at finalization as an aggregate position; nobody needs to claim for the branch to be repaired, winners' ETH is never tied up beyond the lot price, and each bid's vault is a clone rather than a full contract deployment.
  • Fairer exits and refunds. A cohort queue serves earlier requests first and cannot be jumped; provably losing bids get their ETH back before closing, in one transaction if the bidder wants.
  • Bounded work everywhere. Trees, cumulative indexes and immutable fork snapshots keep every transaction's gas logarithmic in participants and constant in questions.

10. Issues, risks and accepted limits

Some of these are stated in the pull request as accepted limits; others are observations about the design. None of them is hidden by the implementation, but all of them matter for anyone deciding whether to use a portfolio.

10.1 Economic

  • Weak-demand capture. When the auction target is missed, all inventory is sold to the bidders present. A lone bidder can acquire the entire fresh inventory for one lot of ETH, then retire and withdraw surplus REP if spare capacity exists. Selling every REP does not pledge every REP.
  • Branches rarely reopen. A branch accepts new purchases only when its cash covers every inherited unit and it holds no recovery REP. Any unsold auction inventory, even after a full ETH repair, keeps it in runoff until the claims owning that REP have been redeemed.
  • No minimum holding period for winners. The branch is repaired by the ETH, but the REP sold can leave the ledger as soon as ordinary vault rules allow. The enforceable backing is the accepted commitment at the chosen health target.
  • Uniform pricing is not truthful. Large bidders still have a demand-reduction incentive. The auction also has no seller reserve, so there is no floor on the price at which REP is sold.
  • Whole-lot rounding. A marginal bid's allocation can differ from its fractional share by less than one lot, and a shortfall below one lot stays unrepaired.
  • Keeper profitability is not guaranteed. Takeover awards are in REP, with no ETH bonus; whether a takeover pays depends on gas prices and REP liquidity. The bot requires independent price evidence and will not sell REP for gas on its own.
  • Only the owner cures. Since c07ee07e5 nobody else can cure a distressed position, and since aa5f8efce a cure pays nothing. A position whose owner is absent stays distressed, earning nothing and holding back the healthy capacity its cured remainder would add, until a takeover keeper acts, which then costs the owner the ramped award rather than nothing. Other providers and buyers who would gain from the restored capacity can no longer cure on their own.
  • A requested reduction cannot be withdrawn. Since 4e7f7ba04 a pending retirement request is locked until it is served. An owner who asked to reduce, then sees prices or fees move in their favour, cannot back out, raise or change the amount, and must wait for spare capacity to serve the request; they can set a new commitment only after it is served.
  • The premium is prepaid and not refunded on resolution. The resolution premium is charged for the full 52-day window, and when a question resolves early its unused portion is burned rather than returned; only the fork-time recovery of 5.2 returns premium to a holder. Buyers of a question that resolves quickly pay for reporting time that never happens.
  • Quotes drift. On a question that already has shares, a fee quoted in one block can be slightly too low a few blocks later (4.3); a caller with a budget tighter than the SDK's 1% allowance sees the purchase revert.
  • Unassigned income is destroyed, not reused. ETH from unhealthy or stale intervals and unused premiums is burned. Nothing leaks to new providers and nothing rewards whoever let a price go stale, but the ETH no longer pays for anyone's upkeep either, and providers still lose the income of every stale interval.
  • Fork cutoff forfeiture. An owner who does not choose a branch within eight weeks loses their pledged principal to the branch Truth Auctions. Absent, sleeping or unaware providers are penalized hard.
  • Shared fate within a universe. Providers back every admitted question, including ones they would not have chosen. The admission policy is the only filter, and a custom policy can be arbitrary; the UI labels a custom policy “Custom policy — underwriting unavailable”.

10.2 Oracle and operational

  • Everything depends on fresh, roughly correct prices. A stale feed freezes capacity, withdrawals of pledged REP, cures and takeovers at once. A feed that is wrong by more than the tolerance can still admit or liquidate wrongly; the band only protects against errors within tolerance.
  • Report funding grows with exposure. A sponsor must post one hundredth of aggregate exposure in WETH; for a large portfolio this is a meaningful capital requirement for whoever keeps the feed alive, and since aa5f8efce nothing reimburses it. Price freshness depends on parties who need a fresh price at that moment paying for it; when nobody does, capacity, withdrawals of pledged REP and takeovers wait.
  • Missing or adverse closing quotes cancel repair. If nobody funds a quote covering the week-nine deadline, or REP got cheaper during the auction, every fill is cancelled and the branch activates impaired.
  • Liveness still needs volunteers. Starting branches, closing auctions, splitting out winners, serving retirements and crediting other bidders' refunds are all permissionless but not automatic; a bidder can at least credit and withdraw their own refund in one step.
  • The portfolio pays nothing for reporting. Since 340d802f1 a reporter's only return is the Escalation Game's REP payout (6.5); the pull request accepts that small or abandoned questions can stay unresolved for a while.

10.3 Engineering

  • Size and novelty. Roughly 7,300 lines of new Solidity across 33 contracts and libraries (plus nine new interfaces), with custom AVL trees, persistent maps, modular prefix arithmetic and 128-bit recovery scaling, delivered in one pull request. The author's own validation note ends with “tests and reviews do not prove absence of bugs”.
  • Storage only grows. Refunded bids, retired positions, frozen books and premium schedules are never deleted, and work follows tree paths rather than being constant.
  • Delegate and module sprawl. The capital ledger runs through two delegatecall helpers, the forker through a portfolio delegate, and the hub through nine module deployers. Each boundary is authenticated, but the call graph is harder to audit than the standalone pool's.
  • Hand-assembled bytecode. Vault clones are built from raw minimal-proxy forwarder bytes with 92 bytes of bindings appended and read back with extcodecopy, and the SDK, the UI and the bot reproduce those exact bytes to authenticate a vault. The compiler checks none of it, so the byte layout is now part of the protocol's verified surface and every verifier must change together with it.
  • Native share migration still needs intervening forks. Shares move universe by universe as before; only capital and books skip the per-pool work.
  • Dependency overrides need upkeep. The audit advisories in follow-redirects, glob and minimatch are cleared by the pins of e4ce60108 (section 8), but overrides force transitive versions the depending packages did not choose, so they need rechecking whenever those packages update.
  • Two systems to maintain. Standalone pools keep their vaults, retention curve, approval registry and tick auction; portfolios use the new rules. Documentation, bots and the scanner must describe both for as long as both exist.

11. Assessment and suggested improvements

The preceding sections describe the implemented design at the pinned revision. The proposals below are alternatives for future evaluation, not features of PR #1164 or conclusions that they would improve security without tradeoffs.

11.1 What the design achieves

  • Individual principal accounting. A position's cure keeps its REP, and its takeover award comes from its own backing. Other providers do not pay that award, although they still share oracle, capacity and fork risks.
  • Lazy accounting. Tree paths and cumulative indices replace fund-wide scans for capacity, income, retirements and auction ownership. Costs and remaining bounds are described in section 7.
  • Explicit auction obligations. Winners back commitments on activation, before individual materialization. Provably losing bids can recover ETH early, while potentially winning bids stay bound until closing.

11.2 Risks to evaluate in operation

  • Weak demand and runoff. A lone eligible bidder can acquire the auction inventory for one lot when the target is missed. A branch with outstanding recovery REP remains in runoff. These are possible outcomes under the conditions in section 10, not predictions about the first fork.
  • Liveness incentives. Sponsors pay for price reports without service reimbursement; owners pay for cures; takeovers pay receivers in REP; reporters earn only the Escalation Game's REP payout. Branch starts, auction closes and winner materialization rely on interested users or operators paying gas. Their willingness depends on position size, REP liquidity and transaction costs.
  • Open admission. Anyone can register an eligible question under the Open policy. Registration creates no demand or provider liability by itself. Funded complete-set purchases create exposure, subject to shared capacity and fees; providers cannot individually veto those purchases. The registrant pays the registration transaction's gas.
  • Audit surface. The custom trees, persistent maps, lazy income accounting and fork imports need review together. Passing gas and model tests does not prove economic security or eliminate implementation risk.

11.3 Possible design changes and their tradeoffs

  1. Bound actual REP sold per lot. A seller reserve could limit weak-demand discounts, leaving unsold REP for recovery claims. Capping only a bid's minimum REP would not achieve this: the current weak-demand rule distributes all inventory regardless of that minimum. This proposal requires changing clearing and recovery accounting, and trades the current sell-all policy for a price floor that may reduce participation and ETH repair.
  2. Reopen repaired branches. Separating old recovery entitlements from new complete sets could allow a cash-repaired branch to accept purchases before all old recovery REP is redeemed. New buyers must not acquire old recovery rights or dilute existing holders; claim generations would add accounting and audit complexity.
  3. Pause stale-price vesting. Deferring income instead of burning it could protect providers during oracle outages. It would also change the incentive to restore a fresh price and require rules for deferred income across exits and forks. It should not be treated as a consequence-free extension of stream end times.
  4. Standing fork choices. An owner-selected default outcome could reduce missed-window forfeitures. Adaptive defaults based on other owners' choices would introduce herding and manipulation incentives; any default must preserve the owner's explicit authorization and the fork's deadlines.
  5. Separate minimum sizes. Distinct settings for minimum commitment, retirement steps and auction lots would allow finer tuning, but each must still prevent dust and preserve the auction collateral and queue invariants.
  6. Configurable takeover timing. The one-hour award ramp balances owner response time against receiver incentives. A longer ramp gives owners more time but can delay repair. Competitive receivers may act before the maximum award; when they act depends on gas, collateral costs, REP liquidity and competing bids for the opportunity.
  7. Admission policy choices. Fixed admission is available when providers want a known question set. A bond could price registration or indexing load, but registration is not itself capital reservation, and a question-count cap could let early registrants block later useful questions. Any new restriction needs a concrete threat model.
  8. Cheaper price renewal. Longer validity or lower report collateral could reduce operating costs, but would increase stale-price exposure or weaken correction incentives. Those tradeoffs require oracle analysis rather than only a gas comparison.
  9. Reporting liveness. With the ETH reporting reward gone, nothing in the portfolio pays for the first report on a small question. A holder-posted reward or a share of the resolution premium for the final reporter would restore a payment, but the removed design shows the cost: any ETH reward needs attribution and anti-farming rules of its own, and the premium compensates providers for their exposure during the reporting window, so diverting part of it would move that compensation without reducing the exposure.

11.4 Focused follow-up checks

  • Cure properties. Fuzz removal against unused commitment, the owner's target and minimum-size rules. A residual is fully backed only when the removal covers its deficit; otherwise check the remaining distress and conservation of principal. Cures pay no reward.
  • Income conservation. Check that released, outstanding, deferred and burned amounts reconcile with funded income, including stale intervals and fork preparation.
  • Counterfactual vault addresses. A predicted address depends on the bid number assigned at submission. Explain that sending assets to a prediction before the bid is mined can target the wrong address, or consider a caller-supplied expected bid number with a revert on mismatch.
  • Post-close UI coverage. A seeded closed-auction scenario would complement the open-auction preview with winner materialization, refunds and runoff. It would supplement, not replace, transaction tests.

12. Glossary

Complete set
One Invalid, one Yes and one No share of a question, minted against ETH and redeemable together while the question is open.
Escalation Game
The REP-staked dispute process that resolves one question locally; two outcomes reaching the non-decision threshold force a fork instead.
Escalation haircut
Two fifths of an Escalation Game's binding capital, burned at settlement instead of being paid to the winning side.
OpenOracle
The contestable price-report mechanism from which Statoblast takes REP/ETH prices for solvency checks, never for market outcomes.
Portfolio
A deployment that shares REP custody, commitments, ETH settlement and pricing across many questions in one namespace. Created from a seed pool with an admission policy and an economics configuration.
Capital ledger
The per-universe contract holding provider REP principal and recording each provider's single commitment, health target and earnings.
Principal, free and pledged
REP a provider deposited. Free principal is idle and withdrawable at any time; pledged principal backs the provider's live commitment.
Commitment
The ETH amount one provider agrees to underwrite. Each provider has at most one live commitment per portfolio and universe.
Health target
A personal multiplier of at least 100% applied to the protocol's REP requirement, governing admission, growth, keeping a commitment unchanged and surplus withdrawal.
Conservative price and liquidation price
The accepted REP-per-ETH price adjusted up or down by the oracle tolerance. Exposure-increasing actions use the former; cures and takeovers the latter.
Demand
The ETH of all live question liabilities in a universe. New demand is admitted only up to the healthy commitment; a later price move can push the two apart until cures or takeovers restore coverage.
Spare capacity
Healthy commitment minus demand. It serves the retirement queue first and new purchases second.
Retirement, cohort
A request to reduce a commitment to a chosen remainder, made by setting a lower commitment and locked until served. Requests are grouped into cohorts served in order from spare capacity.
Cure
Removal of a distressed position's unbacked commitment, rounded up and taken from unused commitment, so the remainder is fully backed when the cure removes the whole deficit, which unused commitment, the new value and the minimum rules can prevent. Only the owner sends one, by lowering their commitment at a fresh price (or, with a pending request, by repeating its value), and it never cuts below the new value; a takeover also cures first. It pays nobody.
Takeover
Transfer of a distressed commitment to a consenting, collateralized receiver for a REP award that ramps from zero to the proportional REP over one hour of distress.
Settlement book
The central accounting of every question's units, share supply, finality deadline, fee stream and recovery REP; the portfolio settlement contract holds the ETH.
Units
A question's ETH-denominated claim on the settlement book's cash. Fees convert units into fee cash; shares redeem for cash in proportion to units.
Fee stream
A funded ETH or REP budget that vests linearly to healthy commitments over the configured vesting duration.
Resolution premium
A buyer's prepayment of up to 52 days of holding fee covering the reporting window, earned by healthy providers until the question's finality deadline.
Maintenance reserve
Removed in aa5f8efce. It held ETH from a service share of fees, unused premiums and unclaimed reporting budgets and reimbursed sponsors of accepted oracle reports; those inflows are now burned and sponsors pay for their own reports.
Reporting reward
Removed in 340d802f1. The first purchase of a question prefunded a per-question ETH budget and later purchases topped it up; the prefunded pot is called the reporting budget. It was held as reward shares in the settlement book, and the final reporter could claim it if they had created a new unique leader, within a cap of one fifth of the smaller of the start bond (the game's minimum deposit) and their lead margin (the gap between the two largest outcome balances), converted at the conservative price; the remainder was burned. Reporters now receive only the game's REP payouts.
Shared price feed
The per-universe contract that accepts one REP/ETH report at a time for the whole portfolio and sizes report funding by aggregate exposure.
Branch, fresh inventory, shortfall
A child universe's portfolio state after a fork; the REP of owners who did not choose that branch; and the question cash the branch did not receive through choices.
Truth Auction (branch Truth Auction)
The per-branch uniform-price auction that sells a branch's fresh inventory REP for ETH to repair its shortfall after a fork. It never selects truth.
Lot, grid
The auction's ETH unit, equal to the minimum commitment, and its REP rounding unit, derived from the security multiplier.
Auction vault
The bidder-controlled account created for each bid as a minimal-proxy clone, with the bidder, capital ledger, auction and bid number fixed in its code. It owns the bid's position after split-out and stays usable for later fork choices and asset recovery.
Aggregate position
The single capital position holding all auction winners' REP and commitment until a winner is split out into their own auction vault.
Runoff
A branch whose cash does not cover its inherited units or which still holds recovery REP: claims settle pro rata, but no purchases or holding fees occur.
Recovery REP
Child REP owed to existing claims: unsold auction inventory plus baskets inherited from earlier forks, tracked at 128-bit precision.
