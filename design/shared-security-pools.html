Design explainer · pull request #1164

Shared security pools and uniform-price Truth Auctions

Pull request #1164 lets many Statoblast questions share one pool of REP security and one set of underwriting commitments, instead of each question running its own isolated SecurityPool. It also rebuilds the post-fork Truth Auction as a uniform-price auction of whole ETH lots whose winners automatically become funded capital providers. This page explains what the change does, how the new system works, the math and incentives behind it, and where its weak points are. It closes with the page author's assessment of the pull request and concrete suggestions for improving it.

1. The change in one minute

Statoblast is Augur's market layer on top of the Zoltar oracle. Before this pull request, every question had its own SecurityPool: its own vaults holding REP, its own ETH collateral, its own fee curve, its own REP/ETH price coordinator and, after a fork, its own Truth Auction. A REP holder who wanted to secure twenty questions had to run twenty vaults and pay for twenty price reports.

After this pull request, a portfolio holds REP once per universe in a capital ledger, and every question registered in the portfolio draws on that same backing. A provider deposits REP, picks a personal health target, and authorizes one ETH commitment. A buyer picks a question and an amount; the portfolio checks that total demand (ETH liabilities) stays within the healthy commitment of all providers, mints the complete set (one Invalid, one Yes and one No share per unit of ETH), and charges a fee that is split between providers, a prepaid resolution premium, a reporting budget and a maintenance reserve. Each question keeps its own outcome tokens, its own Escalation Game (the REP-staked dispute process that resolves a question locally) and separate settlement accounting, but ETH custody and fee bookkeeping are centralized in a settlement book.

The pull request also replaces per-vault liquidation with two keeper actions, cure (remove unused commitment, 1% REP reward) and takeover (take over a distressed commitment for a time-ramped REP award), and redesigns the Truth Auction that repairs a branch's ETH shortfall after a fork: bids are whole ETH lots with a minimum REP-per-lot limit, every winning lot receives the same clearing REP amount, provably losing bids can be refunded before closing, in one transaction if the bidder wants, and all sold REP enters capital custody immediately so winners never need to claim. Each bid's vault is a cheap clone of one shared implementation, with the bidder, ledger, auction and bid number fixed in its own code.

Everything is built so that no operation iterates over all questions, owners or bidders: commitments live in balanced trees keyed by health, fees vest through cumulative integrals, and forks copy immutable roots instead of migrating each vault.

2. The problem: fragmented security

In the standalone design each SecurityPool is an island. A vault owner deposits REP into a specific pool and sets an underwriting commitment there. The pool can mint complete sets only up to the sum of its vault commitments, charges an open-interest fee that depends on that pool's utilization, and requires its own fresh REP/ETH price for any solvency-sensitive step. When the universe forks, each pool migrates its vaults one by one and each child pool runs its own Truth Auction, in which winners must come back later to claim a vault.

Three costs follow from that isolation:

  • Idle capital. REP locked in a quiet question cannot back a busy one. Underwriting capacity is wherever it happened to be deposited, not where demand is.
  • Repeated overhead. Every pool needs its own price reports, its own keeper attention and, in a fork, its own migration and auction. The costs scale with the number of questions, not with the amount of capital.
  • Thin markets. Small questions rarely attract a dedicated underwriter, so the long tail of questions is either unbacked or backed badly.

Standalone pools are not removed. A question can still be deployed as an ordinary SecurityPool with the existing vault, fee and auction rules. The portfolio is a second way to deploy and back questions, and the per-question SecurityPool contract now has a bound mode in which it mirrors the central books instead of holding assets itself.

3.2 The contracts

The portfolio is a hub with a small number of modules, each deployed once per portfolio or once per universe. The hub holds configuration and routes authenticated callbacks; the modules hold assets or accounting, never both for the same thing.

Three design rules recur in every module. First, custody and accounting are separate: the settlement book records liabilities, but the settlement contract holds the ETH; the commitment index records positions, but the capital ledger holds the REP. Second, every mutation is preceded by a checkpoint that pays out income earned at the old weights before any weight, price or commitment changes, so nobody can capture earnings that accrued before they joined. Third, work follows tree paths: no call loops over the set of owners, questions or bids.

4. How a portfolio works, step by step

This section follows a portfolio from creation to redemption. Numbers in the examples use illustrative parameters: a security multiplier of 150% (15,000 basis points), a 5% oracle tolerance, a 1% purchase fee, a 10% annual holding fee, a 0.05 ETH reporting budget per question, a 25% maintenance share and a 1 ETH minimum commitment. All of these are configured per portfolio or per deployment, not fixed by the protocol, except where the text says otherwise.

4.1 Creating a portfolio and registering questions

A portfolio is created from a seed: an ordinary origin SecurityPool whose security multiplier and oracle priority fee the portfolio inherits. The creator supplies an immutable admission policy contract and an economics configuration. The repository ships two policies: Open, which admits any question, and Fixed, which admits a sorted list of question identifiers that can never change. Anyone can write another policy; it is consulted with a static call on registration, quotes and purchases, never on withdrawals or recovery, so a broken policy cannot trap funds.

Setup runs in four permissionless stages, each one transaction, so that deploying the settlement book, reporter ledger, fork coordinator, capital ledger and shared price feed stays under the block gas limit. Registering a question afterwards deploys a thin bound SecurityPool and its ShareToken in the portfolio's own namespace, checks the question's end date against the horizon, and records it in the settlement book. Registration moves no capital and creates no per-provider state: a portfolio with ten thousand questions has exactly as many positions as it has providers.

4.2 Providing capital: principal, commitment, health

A provider interacts with one capital ledger per universe. Three quantities describe a provider there:

  • Principal is REP the provider has deposited. It is either free (idle, withdrawable at any time) or pledged to the provider's single live position.
  • Commitment is the ETH amount the provider agrees to underwrite. All pledged principal backs this one commitment; there is at most one live position per owner, portfolio and universe.
  • Health target is a personal multiplier, at least 100%, that the provider applies on top of the protocol floor. It governs admission, commitment increases, surplus withdrawals and cancelling a retirement. The protocol applies only the floor, both for counting capacity and for liquidation; the target binds only the owner's own actions.

Health is measured against a REP/ETH price quoted as REP per ETH, so a higher price means REP is cheaper and more REP is needed. The REP a position must hold is:

requiredRep = commitment × price × securityMultiplier10 000 × healthTarget10 000

Each multiplication rounds up in the contract. The security multiplier is the seed pool's value, floored at 10,500 basis points (105%). The health target is at least 10,000 basis points.

Rather than re-evaluating every position at each new price, the index stores for every position the highest price at which it is still healthy at the protocol floor:

maxHealthyPrice = pledgedRep×10 000 securityMultiplier×commitment

Positions are keyed by this value in a balanced tree, so “sum of commitments healthy at price p” is one tree walk, whatever the number of providers.

Two prices are derived from each accepted oracle report, using the oracle's configured dispute tolerance (5% in the repository configuration):

conservativePrice = acceptedPrice1−tolerance liquidationPrice = acceptedPrice1+tolerance

Admission, commitment growth, surplus withdrawal and capacity use the conservative price (REP assumed cheaper than reported). Cures and takeovers use the liquidation price (REP assumed dearer than reported).

The gap between the two prices is the point of the design. A report that is wrong by up to the tolerance in either direction cannot both admit a position and then make it liquidatable: a position admitted at the edge needs the accepted price to move by a factor of (1 + tolerance) ÷ (1 − tolerance), about 10.5% at a 5% tolerance, before any keeper can touch it.

Authorizing a commitment requires a fresh price and enough pledged REP at the provider's own target and the conservative price. It also requires that the portfolio's aggregate exposure stays within what the accepted oracle report has funded (see 4.7); a provider who wants to grow beyond that can attach their own settled report and is then held to that report's conservative price as a withdrawal floor until a newer canonical report covers the larger total. Raising a live target is likewise price-checked; an atomic deposit-and-set-target call exists so a provider can add collateral and tighten their target in one step.

4.3 Buying complete sets: capacity and fees

A buyer calls the portfolio with a universe, a question, an ETH amount, a recipient and a minimum number of shares, sending the ETH plus a fee budget. Allocation is automatic: there is no choice of provider. The gating constraint is that new ETH liabilities are admitted only while the healthy commitment of all providers covers the total:

availableCapacity = healthyActiveCommitment ( conservativePrice ) − demand

demand is the ETH of all live question liabilities in the universe plus prefunded reporting rewards. healthyActiveCommitment counts active positions whose maxHealthyPrice is at least the conservative price, plus the part of a retiring position its owner has chosen to keep. The rule is enforced when demand increases and when retirements are served, not continuously: an adverse price move can leave outstanding demand above the healthy commitment, and it is the cures and takeovers of 4.6, not a reduction of demand, that restore coverage. Capacity is zero while the price is stale, after the universe has forked, before a branch has been activated, and while the universe's settlement book is in runoff, the post-fork state in which claims settle but no new purchases are accepted (see 5.4).

Minting itself is proportional. A question's shares are a claim on its units of cash; a new buyer receives cash × outcomeSupply ÷ units complete sets, so holders who arrived before fees were charged are not diluted and later buyers pay the current exchange rate. Every mint increases demand by exactly the cash deposited.

The fee has four parts. The first is proportional to the backing value the purchase consumes, which is the cash times the security multiplier, because that is the amount of REP exposure providers take on:

backingFee = max ( minimumPurchaseFee , cash × securityMultiplier10 000 × purchaseFeeBps10 000 )

The second prepays the holding fee for the reporting tail. Once a question's Escalation Game opens, the linear holding fee stops, but providers remain exposed until finality. The buyer therefore prepays a resolution premium covering the longest possible local reporting window, three days of activation plus seven weeks of required-support ramp (52 days):

premium = addedPrincipal × annualFeeBps10 000 × 52 days365 days

The premium is earned by providers only until the question's finality deadline; the unused remainder goes to the maintenance reserve, not back to the buyer.

The third is the fixed reporting budget. The first purchase of a question (or any purchase after the budget has been partly consumed) tops the question's reward shares up to reportingFeeAttoEth; these are complete-set-equivalent claims that the final unique leader of the Escalation Game can later redeem, within a cap. Every purchase pays the full budget amount; whatever is not needed to top up the reward is routed to maintenance. The fourth is the maintenance share of the backing fee:

totalFee=backingFee+premium+reportingBudget maintenance=backingFee×serviceFeeBps10 000+reportingBudget−rewardTopUp providerIncome=totalFee−premium−rewardTopUp−maintenance

Quotes are exact at the current checkpoint; the transaction re-checkpoints, enforces the caller's total budget and minimum shares, and refunds any excess ETH.

4.4 Earning: holding fees and vesting streams

Providers earn from four sources: their share of the up-front backing fee, the resolution premium, a linear holding fee charged continuously from each open question's cash, and the REP surplus that Escalation Games shed (4.8). The holding fee replaces the standalone pools' utilization-dependent retention curve with something a buyer can compute in advance:

feeRatePerSecond = feePrincipal×annualFeeBps 10 000×365 days

feePrincipal is the cash originally deposited for the question's live shares, so the fee is a flat annual percentage of what buyers paid. It stops when an Escalation Game opens, when the question is final, when the question's cash would run out, or while the universe's price is stale or its book is in runoff. Each second charged converts one unit of question cash into fee cash and lowers demand by the same amount.

Income reaches providers in two different ways. Time-earned income, the holding fee and the resolution premium, is credited at every checkpoint to the commitments that are healthy at that moment, in proportion to size. Lump-sum income, the provider share of each backing fee and the REP surplus from reporting, is handed to a fee stream contract that vests it linearly over rewardVestingSeconds. Vesting serves two purposes: it rewards capital that stays, and it prevents a provider who joins just before a large lump sum is credited from capturing income that belongs to earlier providers. The stream contract keeps one cumulative integral per expiry time, so adding a budget or releasing income costs a constant amount of work plus a binary search over outstanding expiries, no matter how many budgets were ever funded:

incomeReleased (from,to) = ∑stream ratestream × ( min(to,endstream) − min(from,endstream) )

Each stream's rate is its integer budget divided by the vesting duration; the integer remainder is paid at the stream's end. Later funding never accelerates an existing stream.

Whether it arrives from a stream or from a checkpoint, income is split across commitments in proportion to size, but only healthy commitments receive their share. Unhealthy commitments stay in the denominator, so their part is not redistributed to others; ETH that cannot be assigned funds maintenance and REP that cannot be assigned is burned. Income that accrues while the price is stale is unassignable in the same way; after a fork, unassignable ETH is burned instead of funding maintenance. Positions carry their earned ETH and REP separately from principal, and a provider claims earnings in one call.

4.5 Exiting: surplus, retirement and the queue

Free principal can be withdrawn at any time, including after a fork. Pledged principal above the requirement at the owner's target and the conservative price is surplus and can be withdrawn whenever the price is fresh and the universe is open:

withdrawable = freeRep + max ( 0 , pledgedRep − requiredRep (commitment,healthTarget,ownerConservativePrice) )

Reducing the commitment itself is a retirement. A provider requests retirement to a chosen remaining commitment (zero for a full exit; otherwise both the remainder and the reduction must meet the minimum). The position moves out of the active tree into a cohort, the queue of everyone who asked in the same period. Retirement is served only from spare capacity:

spare = healthyCommitment (conservativePrice) − demand

Service happens lazily, whenever something else runs a checkpoint: deposits, authorizations, purchases, complete-set returns, cures and takeovers all try to serve the queue first. Returning complete sets reduces demand immediately, so redemptions free capacity for exits in the same transaction.

The queue follows a few fixed rules:

  • Earlier cohorts are served before later ones.
  • Within a cohort, partial service starts from the healthiest positions; a cohort's unhealthy members leave together with it once spare capacity covers the cohort's healthy commitment.
  • A single position can be served on request only if spare capacity also covers every healthy reduction queued ahead of it, and that targeted service is all-or-nothing.
  • Cancelling a retirement requires a fresh price and the owner's target to be met, because the position re-enters capacity.
  • A retiring position cannot increase its commitment, and a provider cannot bypass the queue by authorizing a smaller commitment directly.

The second rule follows from the capacity accounting rather than from a preference: only healthy commitment occupies the capacity an exit releases, so unhealthy commitment can leave without waiting for spare capacity of its own.

4.6 Distress: cures and takeovers instead of liquidation

Standalone pools liquidate by moving commitment and REP to an approved receiver vault with a 5% REP bonus and a staged price request. Portfolios replace this with two permissionless actions that need no approval registry, no staging and no ETH reserve.

Distress is observed, not declared. Every accepted price records which positions are healthy at the liquidation price. A position's distress clock starts at the first adverse observation after its most recent recovery, and no keeper transaction can start, reset or extend it. A deposit at a stale price does not clear distress; only a healthy mutation at a fresh price does.

Cure

A cure removes only as much of a distressed position's commitment as is unused by the portfolio, that is, total commitment minus demand, and only as much as needed to make the position healthy again at the liquidation price with a margin for the reward:

supportedCommitment=pledgedRep×10 000securityMultiplier×liquidationPrice removed=min(commitment,unusedCommitment,max(minimumCommitment,commitment−supportedCommitment1−1%)) keeperReward=1%×removed×liquidationPrice×securityMultiplier10 000

unusedCommitment is total commitment minus demand, counting every position whether healthy or not, so it is at least the spare capacity of 4.5. A cure that could remove less than one minimum commitment without removing the whole position removes nothing. The reward, paid in REP from the cured position only, is capped so the remaining position still meets the protocol floor. If the remaining position would still be insolvent, the keeper earns nothing; if nothing can be removed, nothing happens. Splitting a cure into several steps at one price cannot increase the total reward, because the reward is a fixed fraction of what is removed.

Worked example: a provider pledged 3,000 REP against a 10 ETH commitment at 150%. The accepted price rises to 230 REP per ETH, so the liquidation price is 219.05. Supported commitment is 3,000 ÷ 1.5 ÷ 219.05 = 9.13 ETH, a deficit of 0.87 ETH. The reduction needed with a 1% margin is 0.88 ETH, which the minimum commitment rounds up to 1 ETH (if the portfolio has at least 1 ETH of unused commitment). The keeper receives 1% of 1 ETH × 219.05 × 1.5, about 3.29 REP, and the provider keeps a 9 ETH commitment backed by about 2,996.7 REP, which is healthy again.

Takeover

If a position cannot be cured, because there is no spare capacity or the position is insolvent, a keeper can take over part or all of its commitment. The keeper first performs any possible cure, then receives the position's REP proportional to the commitment taken, scaled by how long the position has been in distress:

award = pledgedRep × commitmentTakencommitment × min ( 1 , now−distressStart1 hour )

The receiver's existing position, the award and all of the receiver's idle REP together must back the combined commitment at the receiver's own health target and the conservative price; the receiver states the most idle REP they consent to sweeping in, and the call reverts if their idle balance exceeds it. REP not awarded stays with the source owner: in a partial takeover it remains pledged behind the reduced commitment, and in a full takeover everything above the award is returned to the source owner as free principal.

Both actions have bounded variants for bots. A bounded cure pins the expected total commitment, demand and price epoch and demands a minimum removal and a minimum reward; a bounded takeover pins the price epoch, the receiver's own health target and the source cohort, caps the receiver's resulting principal and commitment, and demands a minimum award. The transaction reverts if any pinned value changed. Auction winners' positions can be cured or taken over too; the aggregate auction position is first split so that one winner's loss is never charged to other bidders.

4.7 One price per universe: the shared feed

Every portfolio universe has one shared price feed that replaces the per-pool price coordinators for solvency purposes. Anyone can sponsor a report: they fund the OpenOracle game (the contestable price-report mechanism Statoblast uses for REP/ETH prices) with WETH and REP from their own wallet, and the feed accepts the settled result if it is fresh, newer than the last one, economically defensible at the current base fee, and sized for the portfolio's exposure. Sponsors are reimbursed from the maintenance reserve for accepted work, at most once per 480 seconds per universe, up to a gas-based amount and a configured cap.

Report sizing is where price security meets capital size. A report's WETH side must cover one hundredth of the aggregate exposure, and its REP side one hundredth of any auction inventory, so a report cannot authorize more risk than it would cost to correct it:

exposure=max(totalCommitment,auctionExposure,demand) acceptedExposureLimit=(reportWeth−gasFloor)×100

Commitment growth is allowed only while exposure stays within the accepted report's limit. An owner who wants to grow past it attaches their own settled, larger report; the ledger then keeps that report's conservative price as that owner's withdrawal floor until a later canonical report covers the enlarged total. An older, smaller report can never authorize larger risk.

Reports are independent candidates with separate escrows, so a disputed report cannot block a different sponsor from establishing a fresh observation, and a pending report never extends the accepted price's expiry. Accepted prices are valid for the oracle's settlement time plus five minutes on mainnet (one hour on Sepolia), which is why almost every solvency-sensitive action checks price fresh and why the ledger pauses cash-funded fees across stale intervals.

4.8 Reporting, finality and redemption

Each question still resolves through its own Escalation Game: REP-backed balances accumulate behind Invalid, Yes and No after the question ends, the median balance sets the deadline, and a strict leader at the deadline wins locally. What changes is custody and reward. Reporter REP for all questions in a universe sits in one reporter ledger whose per-question roots are copied to fork children without traversal. The irrevocable surplus that the game sheds whenever the smallest outcome balance rises is routed to providers as vested REP income rather than to a per-pool backing pot.

The final reporter earns the question's prefunded reward, but only if they created a new unique leader and only up to a cap tied to the economic risk they took:

rewardCap = min(startBond,leadMargin)5 × 1conservativePrice

The cap is in ETH, converted from REP at the conservative price when the leader changed, and can only fall over the life of a game. If the shared price is stale at the moment the leader changes, the cap is set to zero and, being non-increasing, stays zero for the rest of that game. A same-leader top-up cannot steal attribution; a cheap, reversible dispute cannot extract a large accumulated reward. Unused reward cash, including for questions whose outcome a fork fixed, goes to maintenance.

When a question's deadline passes, the settlement book freezes its units and their pro-rata share of cash (and any recovery REP, the child REP owed to claims after a fork, see 5.4) into a frozen book, so later fee accrual and later fork shortfalls cannot touch finalized claims. Winning shares and complete sets redeem against the central cash; a redemption burns the shares through the question's ShareToken, lowers demand, cancels any unused premium when a question has no live shares left, and lets the ledger serve the retirement queue in the same transaction.

5. Forks and the Truth Auction

A fork is Zoltar's last resort: when an Escalation Game cannot decide, the universe splits into one child per outcome and every REP holder picks the child they believe is true. Statoblast must then move each question's security into the children. In a standalone pool each vault owner migrates per pool and each child pool auctions REP to repair its missing ETH. The portfolio does this once per universe.

5.2 Freeze, choose, migrate

When the universe forks, the fork coordinator freezes the universe's capital ledger, settlement book, premium book and reporter ledger. The freeze checkpoints every fee earned before the fork and records five totals: pledged REP backing, total commitment, question cash, question units and the live recovery REP owed to claims. During the eight-week choice window each owner with pledged principal calls choose once, naming one outcome. The owner's exact position, including its health target, retirement request and remaining-commitment goal, is exported from the parent ledger and imported into the chosen child's ledger, which is still in import mode and accepts only fork imports until the branch starts, then only price reports until it activates.

Cash follows commitment, not REP. A branch receives the share of the parent's question cash that its choosers had committed to underwrite:

branchCash = parentCash × chosenCommitmentparentCommitment shortfall = parentCash − branchCash

Excess personal collateral cannot pull more ETH into a branch than its owners committed to underwrite. The maintenance reserve and the premium book split by the same ratio. Each branch inherits all of the parent's question liabilities, so the shortfall is exactly the cash the non-choosing owners were underwriting.

After the choice deadline anyone can start a branch. Zoltar migrates the frozen backing REP plus live recovery REP into the child as child REP; the branch's chosen backing goes to its capital ledger, and the frozen backing of owners who chose another branch or did not choose at all becomes the branch's fresh inventory. If the branch has outstanding question units, fresh inventory and a shortfall of at least one minimum commitment, the coordinator deploys a branch Truth Auction and hands it the inventory. Older recovery REP from previous forks never enters the auction; it stays with the claims that already own it.

Missing the cutoff is costly. An owner who does not choose within eight weeks loses their pledged principal's branch fallback: that REP is sold in every branch's auction to repair the cash their commitment was backing. Their idle principal and already-earned fees remain separately claimable from the parent ledger.

5.3 The Truth Auction: uniform-price lots

The auction sells fresh inventory REP for ETH to repair the shortfall, and nothing else. It does not select truth: it sells REP of a child universe that already exists. Its design differs from the standalone tick auction in almost every detail, so it is worth stating the whole mechanism.

Units

The ETH side is denominated in whole lots, each equal to the portfolio's minimum commitment. The REP side is denominated in a grid that makes the per-lot collateral divide exactly for any number of lots:

lotEth=minimumCommitment targetLots=⌊shortfall÷lotEth⌋ repGrid=securityMultiplier÷gcd(securityMultiplier,10 000)

At a 150% multiplier the grid is 3 attoREP, so grid rounding is invisible in practice. A shortfall below one lot cannot be repaired by the auction.

Opening

The auction opens only against a funded, fresh shared price whose WETH side covers the branch's maximum possible exposure (chosen commitment plus every target lot) and whose REP side covers the inventory. The opening conservative price fixes the minimum collateral per lot for the whole auction, rounded up to the grid, and the inventory must be able to back at least one lot:

minimumRepPerLot (healthTarget) = requiredRep (lotEth,openingConservativePrice,securityMultiplier,healthTarget)

Bidding

A bid is a triple: a number of lots, a minimum REP per lot the bidder insists on receiving (a grid multiple), and a health target for the vault the bid will become. The bidder pays exactly lots × lotEth in ETH and nothing else: no REP deposit, no token approval, no execution reserve. The minimum REP per lot must at least cover the lot at the bidder's own health target at the opening price, so a winning bid is always a healthy vault on arrival. Bids cannot be cancelled or repriced. Each bid also creates a small auction vault controlled by the bidder that will own the resulting position. The vault is a minimal-proxy clone (EIP-1167) of one shared implementation, published once by the auction deployer rather than by the fork coordinator; the bidder's address, the capital ledger, the auction and the bid number are appended to the clone's own code and read back on every call. Creating it costs less than a fifth of a full vault deployment, the bindings can never change, and the address follows from those bindings and the shared implementation. The implementation itself refuses to be used directly.

A lower minimum REP per lot is a higher price: the bidder accepts fewer REP for the same ETH. Bids rank by that minimum, lowest first, ties by arrival.

Clearing

At closing the engine finds the largest prefix of ranked lots that the inventory can fund at the marginal bidder's own minimum, capped by the target. Equal-price bids at the margin share the last lots by cumulative proportional whole-lot rounding. Then one of two cases applies:

filledLots=max{lotCount:lotCount≤min(targetLots,⌊inventory÷minimumRepPerLot(bid holding lot lotCount)⌋)} target reached: repPerLot=minimumRepPerLot(marginal bid),soldRep=filledLots×repPerLot target missed: repPerLot=⌊inventory÷filledLots⌋ on the grid,soldRep=inventory

Every winning lot receives the same REP amount. When the target is reached, unsold inventory returns to the branch as recovery REP for existing claims. When it is missed, all inventory is sold, so winners receive at least their minimum and usually more; the indivisible grid remainder is credited to winners as REP earnings in proportion to their lots.

Closing is permissionless at the week-nine deadline and is never extended. The quote is tested at the deadline, not at the time of the call, so a late closer cannot pick a better price. Fills are cancelled and all ETH refunded if the branch universe forked again before closing, if no funded fresh quote covers the deadline, or if the closing conservative price is worse for the branch than the opening one, meaning REP got cheaper and the pledged collateral would no longer back the commitments. Cancellation does not block branch activation; the branch simply activates with the migrated cash alone.

Two worked examples

Four bidders face a 10 ETH shortfall with 1 ETH lots at a 150% multiplier and an opening conservative price of 200 REP per ETH, so one lot needs at least 300 REP at a 100% health target.

In example A the inventory cannot fund an eighth lot at Carol's minimum (3,000 ÷ 400 = 7 lots, and seven better-ranked lots already exist), so the auction stops at seven lots and sells everything it has: each winner receives about 428.57 REP per lot, more than they asked for. In example B the target binds, so the marginal bidder Carol sets the uniform price and Alice, who would have accepted 320 REP, receives 400 REP per lot like everyone else.

Early refunds for provably losing bids

Because bids cannot be cancelled or repriced, demand that ranks ahead of a bid can only grow. A bid is therefore permanently excluded as soon as strictly better funded demand alone would fill the auction at that bid's price:

betterLots ≥ min ( targetLots , ⌊inventory÷minimumRepPerLot⌋ )

betterLots counts lots with a strictly lower minimum. Anyone may credit the refund of a qualifying bid before closing; only the bidder may withdraw it, to a recipient of their choice, and may use the ETH anywhere, including a new bid at a better price. The bidder can also credit their own bid and withdraw in one transaction; that call skips a credit someone else already made and pays out the bidder's entire refund credit in the auction, including refunds already credited for their other bids. A new bid that already satisfies this condition is rejected outright. A bid with a provisional zero fill from equal-price rounding is not refundable early, because later equal bids can still change the rounding.

Refunded bids are not deleted. They remain in the auction's balanced tree as permanently zero-fill history, so clearing and fee ownership never need a scan or a deletion, at the cost of storage that grows with every bid ever placed.

5.4 After closing: winners are owners

At finalization the raised ETH goes to the branch's settlement book and all sold REP goes to the child's capital ledger as one protected aggregate position owned by the auction contract. That position accounts for every winner's commitment, fees and later fork exposure. The branch activates immediately; no winner has to claim anything to be counted as backing.

Any owner or keeper can later split out one bid from the aggregate into its own position, carrying its exact REP, commitment, health target, earned fees and distress history. The split is proportional and exact (the grid guarantees it), and it does not grant a new distress clock. Nobody can cure or take over the aggregate as a whole, so one winner's loss is never charged to other bidders. The bidder's auction vault contract controls the position: it can deposit more REP, set a target, retire, claim earnings and make fork choices like any other owner, and sweep returned assets to a wallet in the same call. Before its first owner operation the vault splits out its own position if the bid won any lot, then records a permanent split-out flag so later owner operations skip the auction lookup and the ledger call; if that first operation reverts, the split and the flag roll back together. A losing bid's vault never records the flag and stays usable for recovering assets.

The branch then lives in one of two states. If accepted ETH plus migrated cash covers all inherited units and no recovery REP remains, which requires the auction to have sold its entire inventory, it is fully operational: purchases reopen and holding fees resume. Otherwise it is in runoff: existing claims settle pro rata in ETH and in recovery REP (unsold inventory plus older recovery baskets, tracked with 128-bit precision so partial redemptions and nested forks keep exact ownership), but no new purchases are accepted and no holding fees are charged. New buyers can never capture an old recovery basket.

6. Game theory and incentives

This section asks, for each participant, why they would behave as the design wants, and where the design admits it cannot make them.

6.1 Why REP backing matters at all

Complete sets are fully collateralized by the buyer's own ETH, so in normal operation providers' REP is never used to pay anyone. Its job is the fork. When the universe splits, the ETH a branch receives is proportional to the commitment of the owners who chose it, and the REP of everyone who chose against it is sold in that branch to make its share holders whole. Because every healthy position holds at least securityMultiplier times its commitment in REP value, the inventory a branch can sell is worth, at the pre-fork price, at least the multiplier times the cash it is missing, as long as those positions were healthy at the freeze. If the branch's REP keeps its value, full repair is affordable; if the branch is the one the market considers false, its REP is worth little and the shortfall persists, which is the intended outcome. Providers are thus paid fees to stand behind their belief about which branch is true, and the admission rule that new demand must fit within healthy commitment keeps that promise collateralized at the moment each liability is taken on; between price reports, keepers' cures and takeovers are what pull a drifting position back under cover.

6.2 Providers: fees for risk, with a dial

A provider's income is proportional to commitment while healthy, so the dominant strategy is to commit as much as their REP supports. The counterweight is the health target. A target of exactly 100% maximizes commitment per REP but leaves the position one adverse report away from the cure zone, where a keeper removes commitment and takes 1% of its backing value in REP. A higher target costs capital efficiency and buys distance from that zone. Because the target also gates surplus withdrawal, a provider who sets it high cannot quietly drain collateral later without first lowering it, and the withdrawal itself is then price-checked against the new target. The design leaves this trade-off to each owner rather than imposing a pool-wide ratio, so a mix of conservative and aggressive providers can coexist in one ledger without one subsidizing the other's liquidation.

Vesting is the anti-sniping device for lump sums. Without it, a provider could join seconds before a backing fee is credited, take a proportional share and leave. With vesting, joining late only earns the slice of each stream that vests after joining. Time-earned income needs no vesting: each checkpoint pays only the interval since the last one, to the commitments healthy during it, and the checkpoint-before-mutation rule guarantees that income already earned at the old weights is distributed at those weights.

6.3 The two-price band as a griefing defence

The oracle is contestable, not infallible: a report within the dispute tolerance is accepted. If one price were used for both admission and liquidation, a reporter could post a price at the top of the tolerance, watch providers be admitted at the edge, then post one at the bottom and liquidate them, all within “acceptable” reports. Using the conservative price for admission and the liquidation price for punishment means an accepted report can never do both: the minimum move between the two is (1 + tolerance) ÷ (1 − tolerance). Distress itself is declared by the oracle's observations, not by keepers. A cure is available as soon as a fresh price leaves a position unhealthy, but the takeover award ramps over an hour from that observation, so before the full REP behind a commitment is at stake the owner has a window that no keeper can shorten.

6.4 Keepers: paid in REP, bounded in both directions

A keeper's cure reward is a fixed 1% of the backing value removed, paid only when the cure actually restores solvency and only from the cured position. This is deliberately small: a cure removes unused commitment, so it costs the provider nothing but the reward, and the design does not want keepers hunting marginally unhealthy positions for large bounties. The takeover award can be large, up to all the REP behind the taken commitment, but it starts at zero and takes an hour to ramp, and the keeper must usually bring REP of their own, because the award alone was by definition not enough to back the commitment, so taking over is a capital decision rather than a free option. There is no ETH liquidation bonus and no ETH execution reserve: keepers are paid in REP they must value and, if they want ETH, sell themselves. The liquidator bot shipped with the pull request values REP rewards conservatively, rejects every cure or takeover candidate unless independent REP price evidence agrees, and ships with portfolio exposure limits that default to zero.

6.5 Reporters: a reward that cannot be farmed

A naïve “final reporter wins the budget” rule invites two attacks. A reporter who already leads could top up their own stake to claim authorship of the final state, and two colluding reporters could flip the lead back and forth cheaply to accumulate a large reward. The attribution rule requires a new unique leader, and the cap of min(startBond, leadMargin) ÷ 5, non-increasing over the game, ties the reward to the smallest REP amount the reporter actually put at risk and to the escalation haircut, the Escalation Game's own two-fifths burn of binding capital, so a round trip cannot net more than it burns.

6.6 Bidders: uniform price, no promise of truthfulness

In a uniform-price auction every winner pays the marginal price, which weakens the incentive to shade bids compared with pay-as-bid, but it does not remove it: a bidder with a large share of demand still gains from demanding more REP per lot (bidding a lower price) if that lowers the clearing level for all their lots. The pull request states this plainly: uniform pricing does not guarantee competitive or truthful bidding. The lot-and-grid design is chosen for a different reason, which is that winning bids become healthy vaults with no further action, so the auction cannot leave REP in limbo waiting for claims.

The weak-demand rule is the most consequential choice. When the target is missed, all inventory is sold to whoever showed up, at inventory ÷ filledLots per lot. That maximizes repair and avoids a seller reserve that would leave REP unsold, but it means a lone bidder for one lot can buy the entire inventory for one minimum commitment of ETH. Winners then have ordinary vault obligations and no minimum holding period: with spare capacity they can retire and withdraw surplus REP immediately. Selling every REP therefore does not guarantee that every REP stays pledged; the enforceable backing is the accepted commitment at the chosen health target, no more.

Early refunds make bidding cheaper for losers and let ETH recycle into better bids, but they rely on the fact that excluded bids can never become winning demand again; that is why refunded bids stay in the tree as zero-fill history rather than being removed.

6.7 Choosers: one branch, one position, no hedging with pledged REP

A fork is a vote with capital. Each owner's live principal and commitment go to exactly one child. Idle principal is left in the parent and can be withdrawn and used anywhere, so an owner who wants to hedge must do it with free REP, not with the REP that is earning fees. Cash following commitment rather than REP removes a subtler game: an owner who over-collateralized could otherwise drag more than their share of ETH into a branch by holding excess REP. Not choosing is strictly dominated, because the pledged REP is auctioned in every branch for the benefit of share holders, while choosing any branch at least preserves it there.

6.8 The retirement queue

Exits compete with new purchases for the same spare capacity. Serving the queue before purchases, in cohort order, and refusing targeted service that would skip a healthy earlier request, means a provider cannot be trapped behind a stream of new buyers and cannot buy their way to the front.

7. Why it scales: trees instead of scans

A single ledger serving thousands of questions and providers is only viable if no transaction's cost depends on how many of them exist. The pull request replaces every scan with a tree path or a cumulative index.

9. Benefits

  • Capital goes where demand is. One deposit backs every registered question; a quiet question no longer strands REP, and a busy one no longer waits for a dedicated underwriter.
  • Costs scale with capital, not with questions. One price report, one keeper watch and one fork migration per universe serve any number of questions. Registering a question moves no capital and creates no per-provider state.
  • Predictable prices for buyers. A flat annual holding fee and a purchase fee proportional to consumed backing replace a utilization-dependent retention curve; quotes bound both the total payment and the shares delivered.
  • Individual, not pooled, risk. Providers keep exact ownership of their principal, choose their own health target, and bear only their own cure and takeover losses. Unassigned income is never redistributed to whoever happens to be healthy.
  • Keeper actions without trusted setup. Cures and takeovers need no approval registry, staging, ETH reserve or bonus pool, and bots can sign exact bounds so a changed state cannot be exploited.
  • Griefing-resistant pricing. The conservative and liquidation prices prevent a report within tolerance from both admitting and liquidating a position; independent report candidates prevent one disputed report from stalling the feed.
  • Auction winners are backing on arrival. All sold REP enters custody at finalization as an aggregate position; nobody needs to claim for the branch to be repaired, winners' ETH is never tied up beyond the lot price, and each bid's vault is a clone rather than a full contract deployment.
  • Fairer exits and refunds. A cohort queue serves earlier requests first and cannot be jumped; provably losing bids get their ETH back before closing, in one transaction if the bidder wants.
  • Bounded work everywhere. Trees, cumulative indexes and immutable fork snapshots keep every transaction's gas logarithmic in participants and constant in questions.

10. Issues, risks and accepted limits

Some of these are stated in the pull request as accepted limits; others are observations about the design. None of them is hidden by the implementation, but all of them matter for anyone deciding whether to use a portfolio.

10.1 Economic

  • Weak-demand capture. When the auction target is missed, all inventory is sold to the bidders present. A lone bidder can acquire the entire fresh inventory for one lot of ETH, then retire and withdraw surplus REP if spare capacity exists. Selling every REP does not pledge every REP.
  • Branches rarely reopen. A branch accepts new purchases only when its cash covers every inherited unit and it holds no recovery REP. Any unsold auction inventory, even after a full ETH repair, keeps it in runoff until the claims owning that REP have been redeemed.
  • No minimum holding period for winners. The branch is repaired by the ETH, but the REP sold can leave the ledger as soon as ordinary vault rules allow. The enforceable backing is the accepted commitment at the chosen health target.
  • Uniform pricing is not truthful. Large bidders still have a demand-reduction incentive. The auction also has no seller reserve, so there is no floor on the price at which REP is sold.
  • Whole-lot rounding. A marginal bid's allocation can differ from its fractional share by less than one lot, and a shortfall below one lot stays unrepaired.
  • Keeper profitability is not guaranteed. Rewards are in REP, with no ETH bonus; whether a cure or takeover pays depends on gas prices and REP liquidity. The bot requires independent price evidence and will not sell REP for gas on its own. The 1% cure reward may be too small to attract keepers for small positions.
  • The reporting budget is charged on every purchase. Once a question's reward is funded, later purchases still pay the full budget, which goes to maintenance. The resolution premium's unused portion also goes to maintenance rather than back to the buyer. Buyers of fresh, short-lived questions pay a visibly higher effective fee than the headline purchase fee suggests (about 8% of cash in the worked example).
  • Fork cutoff forfeiture. An owner who does not choose a branch within eight weeks loses their pledged principal to the branch Truth Auctions. Absent, sleeping or unaware providers are penalized hard.
  • Shared fate within a universe. Providers back every admitted question, including ones they would not have chosen. The admission policy is the only filter, and a custom policy can be arbitrary; the UI labels a custom policy “Custom policy — underwriting unavailable”.

10.2 Oracle and operational

  • Everything depends on fresh, roughly correct prices. A stale feed freezes capacity, withdrawals of pledged REP, cures and takeovers at once, and a lead change during a stale interval forfeits that question's reporting reward for good. A feed that is wrong by more than the tolerance can still admit or liquidate wrongly; the band only protects against errors within tolerance.
  • Report funding grows with exposure. A sponsor must post one hundredth of aggregate exposure in WETH; for a large portfolio this is a meaningful capital requirement for whoever keeps the feed alive, reimbursed only within a capped maintenance budget.
  • Missing or adverse closing quotes cancel repair. If nobody funds a quote covering the week-nine deadline, or REP got cheaper during the auction, every fill is cancelled and the branch activates impaired.
  • Liveness still needs volunteers. Starting branches, closing auctions, splitting out winners, serving retirements and crediting other bidders' refunds are all permissionless but not automatic; a bidder can at least credit and withdraw their own refund in one step.

10.3 Engineering

  • Size and novelty. Roughly 7,600 lines of new Solidity across 34 contracts and libraries (plus nine new interfaces), with custom AVL trees, persistent maps, modular prefix arithmetic and 128-bit recovery scaling, delivered in one pull request. The author's own validation note ends with “tests and reviews do not prove absence of bugs”.
  • Storage only grows. Refunded bids, retired positions, frozen books and premium schedules are never deleted, and work follows tree paths rather than being constant.
  • Delegate and module sprawl. The capital ledger runs through two delegatecall helpers, the forker through a portfolio delegate, and the hub through nine module deployers. Each boundary is authenticated, but the call graph is harder to audit than the standalone pool's.
  • Hand-assembled bytecode. Vault clones are built from raw minimal-proxy forwarder bytes with 92 bytes of bindings appended and read back with extcodecopy, and the SDK, the UI and the bot reproduce those exact bytes to authenticate a vault. The compiler checks none of it, so the byte layout is now part of the protocol's verified surface and every verifier must change together with it.
  • Native share migration still needs intervening forks. Shares move universe by universe as before; only capital and books skip the per-pool work.
  • Known dependency advisories. The CI audit runner currently reports pre-existing advisories in follow-redirects, glob and minimatch that are outside this change but must be cleared before the pipeline is fully green.
  • Two systems to maintain. Standalone pools keep their vaults, retention curve, approval registry and tick auction; portfolios use the new rules. Documentation, bots and the scanner must describe both for as long as both exist.

11. Assessment and suggested improvements

Everything above describes the branch as it is. This section is the page author's own view after reading it: what the pull request gets right, what still worries me, and what I would change, from design-level choices down to small fixes. The suggestions are starting points for discussion, not specifications, and each one trades something away.

11.1 What the pull request gets right

  • Custody and accounting are kept apart, and every mutation checkpoints first. The ledger, the settlement book, the fee streams and the premium book each answer one question, and income earned at old weights is always credited before weights change. That discipline is what makes the fee logic auditable at all, and it is applied consistently.
  • Nothing scans. Capacity, fees, retirement service, auction clearing, fee ownership and fork inheritance are all tree paths or cumulative indexes. The gas tests at 64 and 512 bids and the deployment-size checks back this up rather than assert it.
  • Risk stays individual. Personal health targets, per-position cures and takeovers, and unassigned income going to maintenance or being burned mean that one provider's trouble is never socialized onto the others, and that nobody can snipe income earned before they arrived.
  • The two-price band and oracle-declared distress remove the liquidation race. A report inside the tolerance cannot both admit and punish, and keepers cannot start or reset a distress clock; portfolios need none of the standalone pools' approval registry, staged operations or ETH bonus, with no loss of safety.
  • The auction leaves nothing in limbo. Winners are backing the moment the branch activates, provably losing bids get their ETH back early, in one transaction if they choose, and the frozen bid tree keeps clearing and fee ownership consistent without deletions. Each bid's vault is verifiable from its own code once the shared implementation it forwards to is checked against the published artifact.
  • Finalized questions release capacity on their own. Freezing a question's units moves them out of the live book, so demand falls and the retirement queue advances at the next checkpoint, without a dedicated service transaction.
  • Validation is unusually thorough for a change this size. An independent TypeScript model of the auction compared against the contract, a seeded reference model of the commitment index, module size limits and observed deployments are more than most protocol pull requests carry.

11.2 What still worries me

  • The economic edge cases are not corner cases. Weak-demand capture and the long runoff of almost every branch, lasting until its recovery claims are redeemed (section 10), will both happen in the first real fork, not in a contrived one.
  • Liveness is bought with REP and goodwill. Price sponsors, keepers paid in REP, and volunteers to start branches, close auctions, serve retirements and split out winners are all assumed. Keepers spend ETH on gas and are paid in REP, volunteers are not paid at all, and sponsors are reimbursed in ETH only within a capped maintenance budget.
  • Size and novelty. Custom AVL trees, persistent maps, modular prefix arithmetic and 128-bit recovery scaling in roughly 7,600 new lines, delivered in one pull request, is a large audit surface with two delegatecall boundaries inside the capital ledger alone.
  • Open admission is open to everyone. Under the Open policy anyone can register any question within the horizon, and every provider backs it from that moment.

11.3 Larger changes worth considering

  1. Cap the REP a lot can buy. Today the only ceiling on a bid's minimum REP per lot is the whole inventory, so a single one-lot bid under weak demand buys everything for one minimum commitment. A configurable cap expressed as a multiple of the minimum collateral per lot at opening (two or three times, say) would make the branch keep REP it cannot sell at a tolerable price as recovery REP instead of giving it away. The cost is less ETH repaired when bidders insist on a steep discount, which is exactly the trade the design should refuse.
  2. Let repaired branches reopen. A branch is in runoff whenever it holds any recovery REP, and after a target-reached auction it almost always does, so new purchases on inherited questions are effectively closed after every fork. Three options, from smallest to largest:
    • burn or re-auction unsold inventory in a second window;
    • let a branch whose cash fully covers its units reopen, paying recovery REP only to shares that existed at activation;
    • mark recovery baskets by claim generation so new mints never own old REP. The settlement book already keeps per-question recovery indices with corrections, so a generation marker is a smaller step than it sounds.
  3. Pause vesting while the price is stale instead of forfeiting it. Stream ETH that vests during a stale-price interval goes to the maintenance reserve, which pays report sponsors, and stream REP is burned; the provider loses both, and for the ETH whoever withholds reports moves provider income towards the sponsors who decide whether a report is posted. Extending every stream's end by the stale duration keeps providers whole and removes the incentive. The same applies to the reporter reward cap: convert the REP-denominated risk to ETH at claim time rather than zeroing it when the price happens to be stale at a lead change.
  4. Give owners a standing fork choice. Missing the eight-week window forfeits all pledged REP. An owner-registered default policy, such as a specific outcome index or the branch with the most chosen commitment at the deadline, would protect inattentive providers; a late-choice grace period with a penalty would protect them less but keep the vote-with-capital property intact. Either is better than silent forfeiture.
  5. Decouple the three roles of the minimum commitment. One parameter is at once the smallest commitment, the smallest retirement step and the auction's ETH lot. Lot size should be chosen for clearing granularity and bidder access, the other two for dust prevention; a portfolio cannot tune one without the others.
  6. Make distress economics portfolio parameters. The one-hour takeover ramp and the 1% cure reward are constants. An hour is short for a human owner and long enough for a bot to wait out, so most takeovers will be captured by bots at the full award; a longer or two-stage ramp and a configurable reward would let portfolios with different capital bases choose.
  7. Put a bond or a cap on open admission. Registration is free and permissionless under the Open policy, so providers underwrite whatever anyone registers. A registration bond returned at finality, a per-portfolio question cap, or simply making the Fixed policy the recommended default would limit the exposure without a governance process.
  8. Reduce the standing cost of the shared price. A fresh report must post about one hundredth of aggregate exposure as collateral and a price stays usable for thirteen minutes after it settles on mainnet (about twenty after the report is made), so a large portfolio needs a sponsor willing to lock a meaningful amount of WETH several times an hour. A longer validity window for shared feeds, a divider that grows with exposure, or a cheaper renewal path when the price has not moved would all lower that cost; each weakens the oracle's correction incentive and needs its own analysis.
  9. Split future work and document the modules. The next changes of this kind should arrive as stacked pull requests by module. Independently of that, most of the new contracts have no page in the contract reference, and portfolios have only one section in the Statoblast explanation page; a dedicated explanation page seeded from this explainer should replace that section and be linked from it.

11.4 Smaller changes

  • Make the fee parts mean what they say. Stop charging the reporting budget once it is funded, or split out an explicit maintenance charge, so buyers see what each part of the fee pays for. The unused resolution premium could likewise return to the remaining share holders of the question instead of maintenance.
  • Let a takeover receiver name the idle REP to contribute. Today all of the receiver's idle REP is swept in under a cap, so a keeper with a large idle balance has to consent to all of it or revert.
  • Add a recipient parameter to earnings claims. Principal withdrawals already take one; vault contracts and bots could then route income without a second transfer.
  • Let a bidder pin the bid number. A vault's address follows from the bidder, ledger, auction, bid number and shared implementation, and vaultFor predicts it, but the number is assigned at submission, so a bid that lands behind another bidder's receives a different vault from the one predicted. An optional expected bid number on submitBid, or a note that predicted addresses are only safe once the bid is mined, would stop assets being sent to a vault that is never created.
  • Emit a reason when the oracle-reward callback fails or is skipped. The shared feed's settlement swallows a failed callback, and the services contract returns silently when fewer than 480 seconds have passed since the last sponsor reward, when nothing is committed, or when the quoted reward is zero, so sponsors cannot tell why they were not reimbursed.
  • Seed a closed auction in the walletless simulation. The latest revision's simScenario=portfolio-auction covers the open auction; a second seeded state after closing would also cover winner split-out and runoff.
  • Give the keeper a housekeeping mode or pay for housekeeping. The bot flags stalled retirements and discovers auction winners, but nothing pays anyone to serve retirements, close auctions or split out winners; small rewards or an explicit low-priority mode would make those steps less dependent on volunteers.
  • Add conservation invariants and a cure-reward fuzz. The stateful tests could check:
    • the ledger's REP balance equals pledged principal plus free principal plus REP released by served retirements but not yet withdrawn plus unclaimed earnings, across purchases, fees, cures, takeovers and forks;
    • released plus outstanding plus deferred stream income equals funded income;
    • the cure reward behaves correctly around its cap under fuzzed prices and positions.
  • Show the coverage ratio. The Portfolios tab already shows healthy commitment, settlement demand and each position's adverse-interval start, but not the ratio of healthy commitment to demand, and the scanner shows none of these; a single ratio reading in both would make the state in which demand exceeds healthy commitment obvious at a glance.
  • Clear the pre-existing dependency advisories first. A separate pull request before this one merges would leave the audit job green on its own terms.
  • Add a review guide to the pull request. The description is thorough but dense; a short reading order of the modules, which test proves which claim, and a link to this explainer would shorten every future review.
  • Unify the vocabulary. The branch uses demand, settlement demand, funded demand and aggregate exposure for closely related quantities; one glossary entry each in the published documentation would prevent drift.

12. Glossary

Complete set
One Invalid, one Yes and one No share of a question, minted against ETH and redeemable together while the question is open.
Escalation Game
The REP-staked dispute process that resolves one question locally; two outcomes reaching the non-decision threshold force a fork instead.
Escalation haircut
Two fifths of an Escalation Game's binding capital, burned at settlement instead of being paid to the winning side.
OpenOracle
The contestable price-report mechanism from which Statoblast takes REP/ETH prices for solvency checks, never for market outcomes.
Portfolio
A deployment that shares REP custody, commitments, ETH settlement and pricing across many questions in one namespace. Created from a seed pool with an admission policy and an economics configuration.
Capital ledger
The per-universe contract holding provider REP principal and recording each provider's single commitment, health target and earnings.
Principal, free and pledged
REP a provider deposited. Free principal is idle and withdrawable at any time; pledged principal backs the provider's live commitment.
Commitment
The ETH amount one provider agrees to underwrite. Each provider has at most one live commitment per portfolio and universe.
Health target
A personal multiplier of at least 100% applied to the protocol's REP requirement, governing admission, growth, surplus withdrawal and retirement cancellation.
Conservative price and liquidation price
The accepted REP-per-ETH price adjusted up or down by the oracle tolerance. Exposure-increasing actions use the former; cures and takeovers the latter.
Demand
The ETH of all live question liabilities in a universe plus prefunded reporting rewards. New demand is admitted only up to the healthy commitment; a later price move can push the two apart until cures or takeovers restore coverage.
Spare capacity
Healthy commitment minus demand. It serves the retirement queue first and new purchases second.
Retirement, cohort
A request to reduce a commitment to a chosen remainder. Requests are grouped into cohorts served in order from spare capacity.
Cure
Removal of unused commitment from a distressed position, rewarded with 1% of the eliminated backing value in REP from that position.
Takeover
Transfer of a distressed commitment to a consenting, collateralized receiver for a REP award that ramps from zero to the proportional REP over one hour of distress.
Settlement book
The central accounting of every question's units, share supply, finality deadline, fee stream and recovery REP; the portfolio settlement contract holds the ETH.
Units
A question's ETH-denominated claim on the settlement book's cash. Fees convert units into fee cash; shares redeem for cash in proportion to units.
Fee stream
A funded ETH or REP budget that vests linearly to healthy commitments over the configured vesting duration.
Resolution premium
A buyer's prepayment of up to 52 days of holding fee covering the reporting window, earned by healthy providers until the question's finality deadline.
Maintenance reserve
ETH from the service share of fees, unused premiums and unclaimed reporting budgets, used to reimburse sponsors of accepted oracle reports.
Shared price feed
The per-universe contract that accepts one REP/ETH report at a time for the whole portfolio and sizes report funding by aggregate exposure.
Branch, fresh inventory, shortfall
A child universe's portfolio state after a fork; the REP of owners who did not choose that branch; and the question cash the branch did not receive through choices.
Truth Auction (branch Truth Auction)
The per-branch uniform-price auction that sells a branch's fresh inventory REP for ETH to repair its shortfall after a fork. It never selects truth.
Lot, grid
The auction's ETH unit, equal to the minimum commitment, and its REP rounding unit, derived from the security multiplier.
Auction vault
The bidder-controlled account created for each bid as a minimal-proxy clone, with the bidder, capital ledger, auction and bid number fixed in its code. It owns the bid's position after split-out and stays usable for later fork choices and asset recovery.
Aggregate position
The single capital position holding all auction winners' REP and commitment until a winner is split out into their own auction vault.
Runoff
A branch whose cash does not cover its inherited units or which still holds recovery REP: claims settle pro rata, but no purchases or holding fees occur.
Recovery REP
Child REP owed to existing claims: unsold auction inventory plus baskets inherited from earlier forks, tracked at 128-bit precision.
